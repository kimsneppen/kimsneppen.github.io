Epigenetics: how a cell remembers who it is

Every cell in your body carries the same DNA, yet a neuron stays a neuron and a liver cell stays a liver cell, division after division. The difference between them is not written in the DNA sequence but in which genes are switched on, and that pattern must be remembered and copied along with the chromosomes. This memory of cell identity is what we call epigenetics: heritable states of gene expression that survive without any change in the underlying sequence.

Three ways to remember

Nature uses a few distinct strategies, often in combination. The first is memory by transcription factors: a regulatory protein keeps its own gene switched on, so that once present it stays present. The lysogenic state of phage lambda, held by the CI repressor, is the textbook example. The second is memory written on the nucleosomes, the histone spools that package DNA. Chemical marks on the histone tails, such as H3K9 methylation in heterochromatin or H3K27 methylation by the Polycomb system, recruit "read–write" enzymes that copy the mark onto neighbouring nucleosomes, so a marked region tends to stay marked. The third is methylation of CpG sites on the DNA itself, which maintenance methyltransferases faithfully restore on the new strand after each replication. Real chromosomes typically stack these mechanisms on top of one another.

This applet focuses on the H3K9 system, the mark of constitutive heterochromatin. It is the mechanism behind the silent mating-type region of fission yeast studied in ref. 1 and 6, and the same kind of nucleosome-based memory is what lets each developing olfactory neuron pick and then remember a single receptor gene out of more than a thousand (ref. 2).

Nucleosome modification as a bistable memory (refs. 1, 3, 4)

Chromosomal regions can adopt stable and heritable alternative states resulting in bistable gene expression without changes to the DNA sequence. Such epigenetic control is often associated with alternative covalent modifications of histones. The stability and heritability of the states are thought to involve positive feedback where modified nucleosomes recruit enzymes that similarly modify nearby nucleosomes. Ref. 1 developed a simplified stochastic model for dynamic nucleosome modification based on the silent mating-type region of the yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe, and showed that the mechanism can give strong bistability that is resistant both to high noise due to random gain or loss of nucleosome modifications and to random partitioning upon DNA replication. Robust bistability required (1) cooperativity, the activity of more than one modified nucleosome, in the modification reactions, and (2) that nucleosomes occasionally stimulate modification beyond their neighbour nucleosomes, arguing against a simple continuous spreading of nucleosome modification.

Adding the third dimension: Swi6 and chromatin condensates (ref. 6)

The above model can be extended to include chromatin structure, which is believed to be an important aspect of epigenetic inheritance: the post-translational modification of histones, the three-dimensional organisation of nucleosomal DNA and the interactions between chromatin components all feed into one another. To investigate this interplay, ref. 6 employed molecular dynamics simulations that integrate polymer modelling with enzymatic reactions on nucleosomes catalysed by read–write enzymes. It explicitly models the diffusible heterochromatic nucleosome reader Swi6 (an HP1 homolog) that engages in homotypic and heterotypic interactions, undergoes and induces conformational changes upon binding to heterochromatic nucleosomes, and facilitates the targeted recruitment of histone deacetylases. The model revealed bistable epigenetic states in which the silent state is associated with phase separation of the heterochromatic domain into a condensate; this is what the present applet lets you watch. Condensates were supported by Swi6 in a dosage-dependent manner that can be recapitulated in an analytic mean-field approximation, and a heterochromatic region can induce silencing of another region through spatial proximity in three dimensions.

Model notes

1. What you are looking at

Picture a necklace of beads floating in a little box of water. The beads are nucleosomes, the spools around which DNA is wound, and the necklace is a short stretch of a chromosome with L of them. Water molecules kick the beads at random, so the necklace wriggles and folds. Around it swarm small grey spheres, the protein Swi6, which drift in from outside, wander about, and drift out again. Nothing here is drawn to scale, but everything is drawn to logic: every colour you see means a state, and every state has rules for how it changes.

2. Three colours of nucleosome, and how they talk

Each bead wears one of three colours. A (blue) is the active mark, an acetylated histone that lets genes be read. M (red) is the silent mark, a methylated H3K9 tail. U (yellow) is neither: a bare, unmodified nucleosome. Marks are not static decorations; they recruit enzymes that write the same mark onto other nucleosomes, and enzymes that erase the opposite mark. In the model this is expressed as encounters: whenever two nucleosomes come within distance 2 of each other, an A can erase an M to U (rate rec1), an A can convert a U into another A (rate rec2), and an M can convert a U into another M (also rec2).

Two things make this more than a simple chain reaction. First, going from A to M always takes two steps, via U, so a lone modified nucleosome cannot flip its neighbourhood by itself; it needs allies. That is the cooperativity that ref. 1 showed to be essential for a stable memory. Second, "neighbour" means neighbour in space, not along the chain. When the polymer folds, nucleosomes far apart in sequence touch, and the marks jump across the fold. This is what lets the whole region act as one coherent domain rather than a row of dominoes.

3. Swi6: a reader that turns into glue

Swi6 (the fission yeast cousin of HP1) reads the M mark. In the model a neutral Swi6 (grey, translucent) is just a fast random walker; it feels no forces and is invisible to the polymer. The moment it comes within distance 2 of an M nucleosome it changes conformation and becomes Swi6* (green, "charged"). A charged Swi6 does two things. It becomes sticky: Swi6* attracts other Swi6* and attracts M nucleosomes, so the silent part of the polymer gets glued together into a dense droplet, a condensate. And it recruits deacetylases, so any A nucleosome within reach is stripped to U at rate rec2. Charge is lost at rate decay; a Swi6 that wanders out of the box is forgotten, and fresh neutral ones arrive at rate conc. In this way a modest amount of Swi6 can turn a scattered set of M marks into a compact, self-protecting heterochromatin domain: the marks attract the glue, and the glue keeps the marks close, which lets them defend and spread themselves.

4. Forces and motion

Every pair of force-carrying particles (nucleosomes and Swi6*) that are Gabriel neighbours, meaning no third particle sits inside the sphere spanned by the pair, interact with the potential V(x) = exp(−βx) − exp(−βx/2) when the pair is attracting, and with the repulsive part exp(−βx) alone otherwise. With β = 2·ln 2 the well bottom sits exactly at distance 1, so "touching" always means distance 1 whatever the attraction. Attraction is switched on for Swi6*–Swi6* and Swi6*–M pairs, and optionally for M–M pairs; the Swi6* attraction control scales the whole Swi6* well by ×1 or ×2, in units of the M–M depth. Consecutive nucleosomes are joined by harmonic springs of rest length 1 and stiffness k. All particles move by overdamped Langevin dynamics at temperature T (Swi6 hotter by the mobility factor, since they are small), the nucleosomes are softly confined to the box, and the polymer's centre of mass is pinned to the centre so the turntable always shows it.

5. Noise, time scales and memory

A memory is only interesting if it can be lost. Each nucleosome flips spontaneously at rate spontaneous: A or M drop to U, and U picks A or M at random. This is the thermal noise the epigenetic state must survive. The rate slowdown f divides rec1, rec2 and the spontaneous rate by f, slowing the chemistry relative to polymer motion and Swi6 turnover; the polymer then has time to fold around its marks before they change, which deepens the two basins. With cell division switched on, every cellDiv time units each nucleosome is replaced by a blank U with probability ½ and all Swi6 are washed away, mimicking replication: a true epigenetic memory has to rebuild itself from half its marks.

6. The four initial conditions

A and B share every parameter (f = 2, no M:M attraction, Swi6* ×1, conc 5.5) and differ only in where they start: A fully active, B fully silent. At f = 2 neither state is locked, so A relaxes into the silent condensate and B melts into the active coil. Because the random numbers are seeded (RNG seed), these switches recur at the same times: with seed 1, A goes silent near t ≈ 450 and B goes active near t ≈ 900. C and D are the locked pair: identical to each other, f = 4 with less noise, started active and silent respectively, and each holds its state indefinitely. Same physics, two different fates: that is bistability, and hence memory.

7. Reading the time courses

Open Time courses below the display. The upper trace shows the fraction of M (red) and A (blue) nucleosomes; a switch appears as the two curves crossing. The lower trace is the polymer's radius of gyration Rg, the RMS distance of nucleosomes from their centre. Watch how a drop in Rg accompanies silencing: the condensate is the silent state made visible as shape.

8. How the applet is kept fast

Forces are evaluated only between Gabriel neighbours found on a cell grid, capped at 20 nearest candidates, and the neighbour graph is rebuilt only when a particle has moved more than a Verlet skin of 0.25 (or after 20 steps). Neutral Swi6 are pure diffusers and excluded from the graph entirely; their charging is checked every step against a grid of M nucleosomes so it is never delayed. All spheres and bonds are drawn as GPU-instanced meshes, so rendering cost hardly depends on the number of Swi6. The Speed menu sets how many simulation steps are taken per drawn frame; turn shadows off on a slow device.
