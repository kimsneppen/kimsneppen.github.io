▸ About the models & the game

The sandpile. Grains are dropped on an L×L lattice with open edges. A site holding more than its critical number topples, passing grains to its neighbours and possibly setting them off in turn. Left to itself the pile drives towards a critical state in which avalanches come in all sizes — self-organized criticality.

BTW (zc=3) — a site above 3 grains sheds 4, one along each link; deterministic and abelian.
Manna(4) — same threshold, but each of the 4 grains hops to a randomly chosen neighbour, so a neighbour may receive anything from 0 to 4. Manna(2) — the same stochastic rule at threshold 1: a site holding 2 folders already topples, sending both to randomly chosen neighbours.
Zc=1 rotor pile — every site carries a dark grey arrow pointing at where its next grain will go. A site holding more than one grain sheds its two lowest grains one at a time; each leaves along the arrow, which then turns 90° clockwise. Apart from where grains are added, the dynamics are entirely deterministic — the Eulerian-walker model.
Nem1 rotor pile — the nematic counterpart at threshold 1. Each site carries a bi-directional arrow with a broad end and a narrow end. The grain on top of the pile leaves by the broad end and the one below it by the narrow end; the arrow then flips through 180°, so broad and narrow swap and the next toppling deals the pair out the other way round. Nothing about where earlier grains came from is needed — the glyph itself carries the whole state.

The Grassberger office retells the same rule as a room of clerks. Each desk holds a stack of folders; when a clerk’s pile passes the critical number he stands up in frustration and shoves one folder onto each neighbouring desk, then sits down pleased with himself. Folders crossing the boundary sail out of the open windows.

A and S probe a frozen configuration: S[p] is the size of the avalanche you would get by seeding site p, and A is the total number of topplings at each site summed over all those probe avalanches.

The game. Two to four players share one pile. Every grain keeps the colour of whoever added it and travels with that colour through every toppling; grains already on the lattice when play begins are grey and belong to nobody. A site is controlled by whoever holds the strict majority of the coloured grains on it. A move is: optionally set or turn one arrow on a site you control, then drop one grain on any site at all. The grains that topple away are taken off the top of the pile and then downwards, one per grain distributed. You score a point for every grain belonging to another player that your own avalanche pushes off the open boundary — what somebody else pushes out is their point, not yours, so in a three- or four-handed game each grain that leaves is credited once and only once. First to the winning number takes it. With win at its end point X the game instead runs until the last grey grain is off the board, and the best score then wins; on a level score the game enters ⚡ sudden death — play continues, and the first player to take the outright lead wins immediately.

The computer opponents. At most one seat — Player 2 — is played by the machine, at the level the CPU switch shows. In the Arrow game every level considers, at each step of its forecast, every folder drop combined with every allowed turn of the arrows — an arrow may only be turned on a site that no opponent controls, judged on the board as it stands at that step of the look-ahead. When the exact product of options would overrun the level’s search budget, the arrow lists are sampled down; the folder drops are always searched in full. CPU1 tries every move of its own AND replays every reply of the opponent, and plays the move with the best points-difference after the opponent’s best answer; with 3 or 4 players it instead lets every opponent in turn play their own best answer on the evolving board, their points against each other fully included. CPU2 looks two full rounds ahead — its move, the reply, its follow-up, the final answer (four plies; with 3–4 players: both opponent rounds). With 3 or 4 players the ladder deliberately stops here: a deeper search would have to predict how the rivals play against each other, and beyond two rounds that forecast is mostly noise bought at a steep price — so CPU3 and CPU4 are 2-player levels. CPU3 looks three full rounds ahead — itself, opponent, itself, opponent, itself, opponent, six plies in all combinations. It is deliberately slow on the larger boards; it is offered in the 2-player game only. CPU4 looks four full rounds ahead — eight plies in all combinations, the deepest level of all. The tree grows so fast that it is offered only on the small 2-player boards L = 3 and 4, in the Folders and the Arrow game alike (on L = 5 and 7 the switch tops out at CPU3). The deep levels share a hard budget of 10 million simulated avalanches per move. Typical thinking times in the Arrow game: CPU3 takes about 0.3 s on L = 3, 2–4 s on L = 4, ~8 s on L = 5 and ~18 s on L = 7; CPU4 about 2–3 s on L = 3 and ~7 s on L = 4 — occasionally longer for either in a tangled midgame. Every level — from CPU1 up to CPU4 — obeys the same two absolutes: a move that wins the game on the spot is played at once, and a move that would end the game with a rival in front, or hand any other player an immediately winning reply, is excluded outright — under a numeric target, on the X game’s grey clock, and in sudden death alike. On equal value the drop farthest from the boundary is preferred.
