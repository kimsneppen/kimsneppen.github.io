The Bak–Sneppen model

L species sit on a ring. Each species i carries a fitness barrier B(i) ∈ [0,1] — the height of the barrier it must cross to mutate into a new species. Barriers are drawn uniformly at random.

Mutation is an activated process with rate r(i) = exp(−B(i)/μ), so the waiting time of site i is drawn as Δt(i) = −ln(ui)/r(i) with ui uniform on (0,1]. In each Gillespie step the site with the smallest Δt fires; that species and its two nearest neighbours are replaced by fresh random barriers (periodic boundaries), the clock advances by that Δt, and all waiting times are redrawn. For μ → 0 the smallest barrier always wins and the model reduces to pure extremal dynamics: locate min B, replace it and its two neighbours.

Units of time. At μ = 0 time is counted in updates and a slice is recorded every n updates. At μ > 0 the clock is the real Gillespie time, advancing in wildly unequal jumps Δt ≈ eB/μ; a slice is then recorded every fixed interval of time, so the depth axis stays linear in time and stasis shows up as depth. The two sliders relabel themselves: number of slices, and the total time window. Moving μ across zero changes the unit of time, so the clock, the ages and the event record restart there (the barrier configuration is kept).

Although nothing is tuned, the system drives itself into a self-organised critical state: the barrier distribution becomes uniform above fc ≈ 0.6670 and empty below, except for the avalanches that punch below it. Avalanche sizes and durations are power-law distributed — long stasis interrupted by bursts, i.e. punctuated equilibrium.

Reading the plots

Species i = 1…L runs along the horizontal axis, time runs out of the screen toward you; the time axis carries numbered ticks and an arrow pointing at the newest slice.

Barriers. Vertical axis = B(i), upside down (0 top, 1 bottom), ticked every 0.1. The flip vertical axis button turns both this axis and the age axis the other way round, so the smallest B and the youngest species sit at the bottom.

Age. The height plotted for a species is the age it reached — its full lifetime — so it stays constant for as long as that species occupies the site and steps only when the species is replaced. Species still alive are drawn at their current age. The axis is inverted (short-lived on top). At μ = 0 it runs from 0 to a rounded bound on the largest lifetime in view; at μ > 0 from 1 to the largest age ever reached in the run.

Selected site vs time (second entry in the pull-down). Hides the barrier landscape and plots just the site that fired at each step against time, coloured by the barrier it had, in the same projection as the space–time plot; a button switches to a flat orthographic view from directly above. This is the classical avalanche picture: activity localises in bursts that wander along the chain.

Boundaries. On a ring the chain is periodic, so an avalanche that runs off one edge reappears at the other and the activity seems to jump across the plot. With closed ends the two end sites have only one neighbour inside the system, so a firing end site updates itself and that single neighbour — the activity stays where it is.

Origination–extinction. Every replaced species contributes one event: the time it appeared against the time it was wiped out, binned on a 50 × 50 grid of the displayed time range and drawn as a 3D histogram you can turn. The flat button views it from infinitely far above (orthographic, no perspective) — a plain density map. Both time axes are always linear; only the event counter switches linear/logarithmic, and the bar in the lower right gives its scale.

Time-axis modes

The depth axis is placed by the stored slice times, so it is linear in time and always fills the display; changing the number of slices or the window keeps the run going and keeps what has already been recorded. Each view has a PNG button next to the pull-down, and the two space–time views carry a quick switch between count time (μ = 0, time in updates) and time (μ = 0.01), which runs the finite-mutation-rate simulation on the Gillespie clock.

Window — a rolling window of the chosen number of slices. Full turnover — the depth axis reaches back to the moment the oldest species still present was born, i.e. as far as one can go while every barrier now on screen is younger than the far edge. It follows that instant as the population turns over.
