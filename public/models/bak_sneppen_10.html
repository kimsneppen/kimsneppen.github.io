The Bak–Sneppen model

L species sit on a ring. Each species i carries a fitness barrier B(i) ∈ [0,1] — the height of the barrier it must cross to mutate into a new species. Barriers are drawn uniformly at random.

Mutation is an activated process with rate r(i) = exp(−B(i)/μ), so the waiting time of site i is drawn as Δt(i) = −ln(ui)/r(i) with ui uniform on (0,1]. In each Gillespie step the site with the smallest Δt fires; that species and its two nearest neighbours are replaced by fresh random barriers (periodic boundaries), the clock advances by that Δt, and all waiting times are redrawn. For μ → 0 the smallest barrier always wins and the model reduces to pure extremal dynamics: locate min B, replace it and its two neighbours.

Units of time. At μ = 0 time is counted in updates and a slice is recorded every n updates. At μ > 0 the clock is the real Gillespie time, advancing in wildly unequal jumps Δt ≈ eB/μ; a slice is then recorded every fixed interval of time, so the depth axis stays linear in time and stasis shows up as depth. The two sliders relabel themselves: number of slices, and the total time window. Moving μ across zero changes the unit of time, so the clock, the ages and the event record restart there (the barrier configuration is kept).

Although nothing is tuned, the system drives itself into a self-organised critical state: the barrier distribution becomes uniform above fc ≈ 0.6670 and empty below, except for the avalanches that punch below it. Avalanche sizes and durations are power-law distributed — long stasis interrupted by bursts, i.e. punctuated equilibrium.

The two switches above the plot

The first switch picks what is drawn: B, Age lin or Age log give the barrier or the age of every species as a function of time, with the age axis and the colours calibrated linearly or logarithmically; Active site shows only the site that fired at each step; Orig.–Ext. replaces the landscape by the origination–extinction histogram. The colour bar next to it always carries the scale of the quantity on display — its low, middle and high value, so the middle number is where the linear and the logarithmic calibration part company.

The second switch picks how it is drawn. 2D looks straight down on species × time from infinitely far away (orthographic, no perspective), so the chosen quantity is carried by colour alone and the value axis, being edge-on, is left out. 3D lifts it into the value axis, opening at azimuth 36° and tilt 45° and with the species axis compressed by 30 %, which is what makes room for the depth; the origination–extinction histogram is square and is not compressed, and it opens at azimuth −67°, tilt 40°. From there the mouse, a drag, the two angle sliders and the wheel or a pinch take over. Either way the framing is computed from the window — the whole figure, axes, numbers and labels included, stays inside the frame on a phone as well as on a desktop, pushed up against the control column. Reset view hands the framing back to the applet after you have panned.

Reading the plots

Species i = 1…L runs along the horizontal axis, time runs out of the screen toward you; the time axis carries numbered ticks and an arrow pointing at the newest slice.

Barriers. Vertical axis = B(i), upside down (0 top, 1 bottom), ticked every 0.1. The flip vertical axis button turns both this axis and the age axis the other way round, so the smallest B and the youngest species sit at the bottom.

Age. The height plotted for a species is the age it reached — its full lifetime — so it stays constant for as long as that species occupies the site and steps only when the species is replaced. Species still alive are drawn at their current age. The axis is inverted (short-lived on top). At μ = 0 it runs from 0 to a rounded bound on the largest lifetime in view; at μ > 0 from 1 to the largest age ever reached in the run.

Active site. Hides the barrier landscape and plots just the site that fired at each step against time, coloured by the barrier it had. This is the classical avalanche picture: activity localises in bursts that wander along the chain.

Boundaries. On a ring the chain is periodic, so an avalanche that runs off one edge reappears at the other and the activity seems to jump across the plot. With closed ends the two end sites have only one neighbour inside the system, so a firing end site updates itself and that single neighbour — the activity stays where it is.

Origination–extinction. Every replaced species contributes one event: the time it appeared against the time it was wiped out, binned on a 50 × 50 grid of the displayed time range and drawn as a 3D histogram you can turn; 2D views it orthographically from infinitely far away — a plain density map. Both time axes are always linear. The vertical axis carries no numbers, because the colour key in the plot already gives the scale of the count per bin; only that counter switches between a linear and a logarithmic scale.

Why count time gives a flat diagonal. A species is born when its site is refreshed and dies when that site is refreshed again, so the dot sits off the diagonal by exactly its lifetime — often long, since three fresh barriers may all come out high and then wait. But count the events in one bin of the extinction axis: every update replaces three species, so that bin holds exactly 3 × (bin width) events wherever it sits, and of those exactly L — the species in flight at the bin edge, no more and no fewer — were born in an earlier bin. Bin the whole run and that is L = 100 against 3 × 3090 = 9270: 1 % of the events off the diagonal, and a ridge flat to a fraction of a per cent. It is an identity, not a defect — in count time the clock is the event counter, so the event rate is uniform by construction.

Two things follow. Shortening the window does not make the diagonal interesting, but it does lift the off-diagonal band from 0.7 % to about 12 % of the events, so in count time the window defaults to one mean lifetime per bin (the header reports it) until you move the slider. And the punctuation is only visible on the Gillespie clock, where waiting times span e^(1/μ): there the per-bin totals vary by a factor of several instead of a fraction of a per cent, and the plot fills with voids and dense patches. That is what the clock switch above the plot is for. Note that at μ = 0.01 the model's own dynamic range, e^100, is far beyond double precision, so once the clock has taken a large jump the tiny steps inside an avalanche no longer register in it at all — those lifetimes read as exactly zero, which on that clock is what they physically are.

Time-axis modes

The depth axis is placed by the stored slice times, so it is linear in time and always fills the display; changing the number of slices or the window keeps the run going and keeps what has already been recorded. A PNG button saves whatever is on screen, and the space–time views carry a third switch between count time (μ = 0, time in updates) and time (μ = 0.01), which runs the finite-mutation-rate simulation on the Gillespie clock.

Window — a rolling window of the chosen number of slices. Full turnover — the depth axis reaches back to the moment the oldest species still present was born, i.e. as far as one can go while every barrier now on screen is younger than the far edge. It follows that instant as the population turns over.
