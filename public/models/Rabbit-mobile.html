Foxes and Snakes eating Rabbits: See about »

Classical predator-prey models limit the coexistence of different predators due to competitive exclusion [1, 2]. However, real ecosystems contain interactions that go beyond mere predation. Here, we propose to extend these systems by allowing for plants that allow for shelters. We explore the interaction rules that make plants facilitate biodiversity.

1. The cast: four species on a chessboard

The world is a square grid of sites (a lattice) that wraps around at the edges, like the map in an old video game. Four kinds of things live on it. Plants (green trees) cover some sites and spread slowly to neighbouring sites. Rabbits (grey) hop between neighbouring sites, breed, and eat nothing in the model — they are simply food. Foxes (orange) run around and eat rabbits, but only rabbits standing in the open. Snakes (yellow in the 3-D scene) also roam, but they only eat rabbits that are hiding under a plant. So the plant is both a shelter and a trap: it protects a rabbit from foxes but exposes it to snakes.

2. Why two predators normally cannot share one prey

In textbook ecology, two predators competing for exactly the same food usually cannot coexist: whichever one is slightly better at catching rabbits will eventually crowd the other out. This is called competitive exclusion. Our twist is that foxes and snakes do not hunt the same rabbits — foxes hunt the exposed ones, snakes hunt the hidden ones. The plants split one prey into two "kinds", and that can be enough to let both predators survive. The question the model asks is: which rules about plants, rabbits and predators make this coexistence work?

3. How one time step works

Time advances in small steps of length Δt. In each step every plant, rabbit, fox and snake gets a chance to act, with probabilities set by the rates in the control panel. A plant spreads to a random neighbouring site with probability γσΔt. A rabbit tries to hop to a random neighbour (μRΔt), breeds a new rabbit onto a free neighbour (εΔt), and — in most presets — may trample the plant it moves onto (probability σ). Foxes and snakes move (μFΔt, μSΔt), die of old age (δΔt), and when they land on a rabbit they can eat, they eat it and with probability f (foxes) or fS (snakes) produce a cub or hatchling. Several foxes or snakes may share one site; a small number badge shows how many.

4. What the rabbits know

The pull-down in the upper-left corner of the display (and the Rabbit shelter button in the control panel) chooses how clever the rabbits are. Rabbits damage and hide: a rabbit never leaves a plant for open ground, so once it finds cover it stays there — and it damages plants when it moves onto them. Rabbits damage and optimize: each rabbit looks at its 5×5 surroundings, multiplies the number of foxes by the fox mobility and the number of snakes by the snake mobility (that product is how often each predator would run into it), and then keeps to whichever kind of site is safer — under a plant if foxes are the bigger local danger, in the open if snakes are. Stupid rabbits hop wherever chance takes them, cover or no cover. Frozen forest, stupid rabbits starts from one round forest covering half the lattice, with plants that neither grow nor get trampled, and stupid rabbits. The control panel also has an adaptive option, which uses the fox and snake numbers of the whole system instead of the local neighbourhood.

5. The six scenario presets

1 · Rabbits clear plants — the default: rabbits trample plants (probability σ) when they successfully move onto one. 2 · Fixed plant cover — plants are scattered at a chosen density and then frozen. 3 · Foxes clear plants — only a fox arriving above a hidden rabbit can remove the plant (this one is bistable: runs end up either with foxes or with plants, rarely both). 4 · Rabbits kill plants — a rabbit erodes the plant it ends up below on every move attempt; the plant growth γ is raised to compensate. 5 · Frozen circular forest — one solid disc of plants at the centre; use the density slider to change its size. 6 · Snakes kill plants — snakes, not rabbits, erode the plants they crawl into. All presets use L = 20, γ = 1, σ = 0.064, μR = μF = 10, μS = 5, f = fS = 0.1, δF = δS = 0.1 and ½ simulation step per animation frame.

6. Reading the plots

The population plot below the scene shows the fraction of sites occupied by each species against time: green for plants, orange for foxes, black for snakes, dark green for rabbits hiding under plants, and a grey band for all rabbits. When any curve hits zero that species is extinct, and the fun is over — so the interesting question is how long all four can be kept alive. For the circular forest a third panel shows the time-averaged density of rabbits, foxes and snakes as a function of distance from the forest centre: you can see where the rabbits crowd, where the snakes ambush them, and how the foxes patrol the open edge.

7. The 3-D scene and the buttons on it

When the displayed lattice is at most 20 sites wide it is drawn as a 3-D landscape: trees and animals on a meadow, and a see-through underground beneath each tree where hidden rabbits crouch and snakes go hunting. The scene always zooms to fill the display; the viewing distance (1.2 … 10 × L) only sets how strong the perspective is. Drag the scene to orbit it, scroll or pinch to change the perspective. The lower-left button cycles four camera presets (3D view A, 3D view B, 3D side, top view); next to it you can switch the background between white and black and save the picture as a PNG cropped tightly around the system. The lower-right corner has small copies of the Start/Pause and Reset buttons and the "steps per frame" setting. At ½ or 1 step per frame you see the animals move: rabbits hop, foxes run, snakes wiggle, a rabbit slipping under a tree lowers its ears as it crouches, a fox that catches a rabbit leaps and holds it in its jaws, and a snake that strikes swells while it swallows. At higher speeds the animation is skipped so the simulation can run fast.

8. Sizes: L10, L20★, L30★ …

L is the number of sites along one side. The starred presets L20★ and L30★ simulate the full 20×20 or 30×30 system but display only its lower-left quarter, so that each animal is big enough to see while the dynamics still happen on a large lattice. L30 and L50 are shown as a flat map instead of the 3-D scene.

9. Things to try

Start with the default and watch whether all four species survive. Then switch the rabbits to optimize — do smarter rabbits help or hurt the predators? Try stupid rabbits: without the hiding rule, do foxes and snakes still both make a living? Lower the frozen plant density in preset 2 until one predator disappears, and raise it until the other does. In the circular forest, look at the radial plot and ask where you would sit if you were a snake.
