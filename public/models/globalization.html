Globalization in a nutshell — 2D

A two-dimensional, many-product version of the model of Sneppen and Bornholdt [1]. L × L agents sit on a square lattice with periodic boundaries. There are N products, each with three parameters: a start cost Start(i), an economy-of-scale exponent γ(i) and a transport cost β(i). Each agent at r owns money and a list F(r,i) = 1 or 0 of the products it can produce.

The price at which agent r₁ delivers one unit of product i to agent r₂ is

C(r₁,r₂,i) = F(r₁,i) · s(r₁,i)γ(i) + β(i) · |r₁ − r₂|   ( + τ across a border )

Only agents with F(r₁,i) = 1 can deliver, and an agent does not buy from itself. s(r₁,i) = 1 + the number of units of i that r₁ sold in the previous round: a new factory has s = 1 and price 1, and with γ < 0 a large producer sells cheaper (economy of scale). |r₁ − r₂| is the shortest distance on the torus, Euclidean or Manhattan.

One round (length T)

  1. Orders. Each product gets n·L² orders. The ordering agent is drawn with probability proportional to the money it acquired in the previous round (wage + sales): rich agents place more orders, which enriches their surroundings.
  2. Sales. An order goes to the agent that delivers at the lowest price C (ties at random). Prices are fixed during the round, so every agent buys each product from the same seller all round.
  3. Money. Each round an agent's money changes by ΔM = w + (units sold) − μ · Σits factories Start(i) A unit sold earns one unit of money (the reward of the original model). The wage w, the same for everybody, is the savings from work that let an agent without a business build up money to start one. The upkeep μ·Start(i) is the running cost of a factory: the investment depreciates, so a factory must keep selling to pay for itself. Opening a factory costs Start(i) at once. An agent whose money falls below zero is bankrupt: its least profitable factory closes and the debt is written off.
  4. Decisions. After the round each agent compares its profit (sales − upkeep) with that of the round before. If profit held up, the agent goes on producing what it did. If it declined by more than the random fluctuation κ·√(sales), or is not positive, or the agent has no factory, the agent explores: it picks at random a product it does not make and has money enough to start, pays Start(i) and opens that factory (s = 1). An agent may make at most lim products; at the limit it closes its weakest factory for the new one, so with lim = 1 it changes product.

Initially every agent makes one random product and owns money M₀. Money is not conserved: the buyers' payments come from outside the model, as in [1], and money leaves the economy through start costs and upkeep.

What to look for

Lowering the transport costs (the transport factor multiplies every β, and lower gradually decreases it each round, as in Fig. 2 of [1]) turns many local producers into a few large companies with long deliveries. Products with a strong economy of scale (γ near −1) and cheap transport go global first; products that are costly to ship stay local. Countries with a tariff τ on crossing a border (only one border counts) split the markets into national champions.

Views

3D: the lattice seen from the side, as in the figures of [1]; every factory is a tower as high as the units it sold in the last round, stacked by product (linear or log heights). Drag to turn and tilt. Agents: every agent with sales is a pie, area ∝ sales, with a slice for each product it makes; the pale background shows the factories that sold nothing. Market: for the selected product, each agent in the colour of the factory it buys from; dots are the factories, size ∝ sales. Sales and Money: log-scale heat maps in the gnuplot palette of the paper's figures. Hover over or tap an agent for its numbers. Below the grid: time series of the number of factories, the largest market share, the mean shipping distance, the agents with sales, starts and bankruptcies, and the mean money.
