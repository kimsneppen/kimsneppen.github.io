Globalization in a nutshell — 2D

how it works

Why is bread still baked around the corner, while nearly every computer chip in the world comes from a handful of factories far away? This applet is a toy world for thinking about that question. It is the model of Sneppen and Bornholdt [1], drawn on a flat map and extended from one product to many.

The town

Picture a square town of L × L people, one on each square of a grid: 125 × 125 = 15 625 people at the start. We call them agents. Everybody needs a few kinds of goods, called A, B and C. An agent can own a factory that makes one of them, and every agent has some money.

Think of A as bread: a bakery is cheap to start, and a big bakery is not much cheaper than a small one. B is an ordinary industry. C is high tech, like computer chips: a chip factory is very expensive to build, but the bigger it is, the cheaper each chip becomes.

The town has edges, like an island. In the control panel (the tab at the right edge of the window) you can wrap it around like a doughnut instead (the edges menu in the upper right corner), so that walking off the right side brings you back on the left. There you can also change the products, add more of them, and change every other setting.

What things cost

When you want one unit of a product, you look at every factory that makes it and buy from the one that is cheapest for you. A factory at a distance d from you asks the price

C = sγ + β · d

sγ is what it costs the factory to make one unit. Here s is the size of the factory: 1 + the number of units it sold last round, and γ (gamma) is a negative number. A brand-new factory has s = 1, so its price is 1. With γ = −0.3 (bread), a factory that is 100 times bigger makes each unit only 4 times more cheaply. With γ = −0.9 (chips), it makes it 60 times more cheaply. Being big pays: this is called the economy of scale.

β · d is the transport: every step between the factory and you adds β (beta). Bread is costly to carry (β = 0.1 per step), chips are cheap to ship (β = 0.02).

Building a factory costs Start: 2 for A, 8 for B, 20 for C. The products that gain most from being big also cost most to start, and the applet warns you if you change the numbers so that this is no longer so. Nobody buys from their own factory.

One round, step by step

  1. Shopping. For every product, orders are placed: n per person on average (n = 1 in the standard setup; the n buttons in the upper right corner of the town give 0.2, 1, 2 or 5). Who orders? People picked at random — but those who earned more last round are picked more often. Rich people shop more.
  2. Selling. Every order goes to the cheapest factory, price plus transport.
  3. Money. Everybody earns the same wage, w = 0.05 per round. A factory owner also earns p for every unit it sold, and can be made to pay upkeep: each round a fraction μ of what its factory cost to build, because machines wear out. The standard setup has no upkeep, μ = 0; the μ buttons set it from 1 to 10 %. ΔM = w + p · (units sold) − μ · Start The price p sets itself, so that everything sold in a round is worth exactly what everybody earned in wages: p = (all the wages) / (all the units sold) If the town makes twice as much, each unit is worth half as much. An agent whose money falls below zero is bankrupt: its factory closes, its debts are forgiven, and it starts again with nothing but the wage.
  4. Deciding. Every owner compares this round's profit with last round's. If things went fine, it carries on. But if the profit fell (by any amount in the standard setup; see Patience below), or there is no profit, or it has no factory, it tries something new: it picks at random another product it can afford, pays Start, and opens a new factory, which starts small (s = 1). An agent runs one factory at a time (you can allow more in the control panel), so the old one closes.

At the start, every agent has money 5 and a factory of a random product. That is the whole model.

Patience: when does an owner give up?

Sales always go up and down a little by pure chance, like the number of heads when you toss a hundred coins. A factory that sells about q units a round sees chance ups and downs of about √q units. So an owner gives up only when its profit falls by more than κ (kappa) times that ordinary wobble: by more than κ · p · √q.

The patience menu in the upper right corner of the town sets κ:

  • 0 (the standard setup): any fall of profit, however small, makes the owner give up. Very nervous owners.
  • 1 (normal): the fall must be bigger than ordinary chance.
  • 2 to 5: the fall must be 2 to 5 times bigger than chance.

With patience 0, 1 or 2 the biggest chip company usually lasts only a round or two: a small dip in its profit is enough for its owner to give it up. With patience 3 it lasts about 8 rounds, and with 4 or 5 it can rule for tens to hundreds of rounds, while the richest agents end up 10 to 25 times richer (measured on a 64 × 64 town with n = 0.2). With n = 5, patience changes little: the sales are so large that even patience 5 forgives a fall of only a few percent.

In the standard setup, n = 1 on the 125 × 125 town, the big chip companies last about four rounds with any patience up to 3, though 3 already doubles the share of the richest 1 % (from 16 to 33 % of all the money in the self-organized economy). Patience 4 or 5 depends on when you choose it. Chosen in a running self-organized town, it lets the rich keep their companies: within 1 000 rounds the richest 1 % go from about 15 % of all the money to half (κ = 4) or three quarters (κ = 5), and the richest agent from about 1 000 to a few thousand (κ = 4) or 15 000 to 40 000 (κ = 5), when a chip giant can rule for hundreds of rounds. The median stays put, and when the buyers pay the change is much smaller. Chosen before a new start (↺), it freezes the town: some 200 small chip factories from the first rounds keep their neighbourhoods, and no giant ever forms.

Where does the money come from, and where does it go?

Every round money comes in twice: as wages, and as payment for the units sold — and the price makes the sales worth exactly as much as the wages. It is as if everybody earned two wages, spent one on goods and saved the other. Money leaves in two ways: when someone builds a factory, and as upkeep, if there is any. On the 125 × 125 town about 1 560 comes in per round (780 in wages, 780 in sales). In the standard setup, without upkeep, about 1 530 of it goes into new factories; with upkeep 0.02, about 930 goes into new factories and 620 into upkeep. Almost nothing stays.

Why does it balance so neatly? The town has a thermostat. Anyone who does badly and has enough money starts a new factory, and most of these start-ups fail: the start cost is lost, and with upkeep they also pay upkeep until they go bankrupt. The more money there is, the more start-ups, and the more money is burned — until what goes out matches what comes in.

When a big company closes, its owner keeps the money and tries one new factory after another. Most of them fail, and the fortune drains away step by step: in the standard setup half of it is gone after about 6 rounds (after about 16 with n = 0.2, patience 1 and upkeep 0.02). That is why a golden money column stays for a while after its company has crashed.

That spending needs a second product. With a single product an owner whose profit falls has nothing to switch to, so it keeps its factory and never pays a start cost again. Then the sales money of the established companies has no way out: on a 125 × 125 town the money grows by about 770 a round, almost a whole wage bill, and after 1500 rounds the richest 1 % own 95 % of it, while the median stays near 4.5. The preset Twin products therefore has two products with the same numbers (Start 5, γ −0.5, β 0.05) but different names. Owners can switch between them, about 150 times a round, and the money stays level, with the richest 1 % owning about 28 %.

Three kinds of economy

The golden button in the upper right corner switches between the first two; the third is in the control panel:

  • self-organized (the standard setup): as described above.
  • buyers pay: now the buyers pay for their shopping. For every unit they pay the price p, which goes to the seller, plus the transport and any border tax, which leave the town (to the carriers and the state): ΔM = w + p · (units sold) − Σunits bought p · (1 + C − sγ) − μ · Start Where does the 1 come from? A unit costs the buyer two things. The good: p, which the seller receives — the same p that appears as + p · (units sold) in the seller's account. The delivery: the part of the price C that is not the cost of making it, C − sγ, turned into money with the same p. Together that is p · 1 + p · (C − sγ) per unit bought. Money is scarcer now: there are fewer start-ups, agents go bankrupt when their shopping costs more than they have, and the big companies win larger shares of their markets. With upkeep the difference becomes much larger (lesson 8).
  • fixed price: every unit sells for 1, as in the original model. Now nothing holds the money back: it piles up — in the standard town the mean money passes 1 000 within 600 rounds — and the poorer half of the agents ends up with almost none of it.

Roads, borders, taxes and tariffs

With the buttons in the upper left corner you draw on the town: choose one, then press, drag and let go.

  • A road makes transport along it cheaper; its ▾ sets how much (× 0.2 means five times cheaper).
  • A border makes goods pay when they cross it. Its ▾ sets a tax, a percentage of what the goods are worth where they cross (their price plus the transport so far), and a tariff, a fixed amount per unit. A one-way border charges only goods crossing in the direction of its arrows; the arrows point to your left as you draw it (drawn from top to bottom, they point to the right).
  • Ready-made shapes, in the same ▾ menus: a road from the centre to a corner of your choice, and a circle in the centre enclosing 5 to 70 % of the town, like a country in the middle of the world. The circle charges goods both ways, or only goods going in: then it protects the inside, while its own goods go out free. The product buttons in the same menu choose which products the circle taxes, for instance only the chips.
  • − road and − border: tap one to remove it. ✕ all removes them all.

Goods always travel the cheapest way: they may follow a road part of the way, or go around the end of a border instead of paying.

A tax hits cheap goods lightly. A big chip factory makes each chip for about 0.02, while a newcomer on the other side of the border starts at 1, so even a 100 % tax leaves the giant far cheaper. A tariff is different: it costs the giant as much as anybody. Across a border down the middle of a 64 × 64 town, chips from the other side reach 19 % of the buyers without a border, and 43 % with a 100 % tax — but only 12 % with a tariff of 0.2, 4 % with 0.5 and 0.5 % with 1. Then each side gets its own chip factory. Bread stops crossing at a tariff of 0.2 to 0.5.

What to look for

The big question: can a newcomer ever beat a giant? The newcomer sells at price 1, the giant at sγ, which can be tiny. A customer goes to the newcomer only if the giant's transport cost has eaten up its advantage. So the giant keeps every customer within a distance of about R ≈ (1 − sγ)/β. If R is bigger than the whole town, no newcomer can win a single customer anywhere. The giant is a natural world monopoly, and it falls only when its owner gives up.

Now you can see what globalization does. Make transport cheaper (the transport factor in the control panel; lower gradually keeps lowering it) and R grows: many small local producers merge into a few big ones that ship far. Products with a strong economy of scale and cheap transport go global first. Bread stays local.

Two more things to watch. The giants are fragile: their owners give them up after a few rounds, because sales and prices wobble. And a bakery can suddenly boom: when its neighbours close, it takes over their customers, grows ten times bigger in two or three rounds, gets rich — and then its owner switches to B or C at the first dip in profit.

How to read the picture of the town

3D (the default): the town seen from the side. Every factory is a tower as tall as the number of units it sold last round. The golden glass columns are the agents' money, hanging below the ground or standing around the factories (menu at the top). The scale bars at the left show the height of a round number of units and of money. Drag to turn and tilt, use the mouse wheel to zoom, or open the view menu. lin / log switches the towers to a logarithmic scale, so that small factories show up too.

Agents: every seller is a small pie, its area the units sold, a slice per product. Market: for one product, everybody is coloured like the factory they buy from. Sales and Money: heat maps. When there are borders, every view shows the mean and the median money in each region they cut out (in 3D written on the ground, behind the towers), so you can compare the countries. Click the numbers to switch all of them to their averages over the last two thirds of the run (in gold), and click again for the present. Hover over or tap an agent to read its numbers.

The buttons in the lower right corner:

  • markets A B C: dotted lines on the ground around the customers of every seller of that product.
  • ⚡ marks every crash, yellow, in the company's colour, or off. The bigger the company, the bigger the mark: a soft flash where it stood, and small lightnings along its tower and its money column. The tower sinks to the ground.
  • ◐ switches between a black and a white background. .png saves a picture with all the settings written under it, sq a square picture of just the town.

The plots under the town

In time: how something changes from round to round — the number of factories of each product (dashed: their effective size (Σs)²/Σs², where s is what a factory sells: the number of equal factories that would be as concentrated — 1 when one company sells everything), the market share of the biggest company, how far goods travel, the start-ups and bankruptcies, the money, the price.

Distributions: how the money is spread over the agents, P(M), and how big the companies are, P(s). Both axes are logarithmic: each step along an axis multiplies by 10. On such a plot a power law, P ∝ x−a, is a straight line, and the dashed lines are such fits. In P(M) the dotted lines mark the mean money (orange) and the typical money, the median (blue): half of the agents have less. The tail is fitted from five times the mean to a fifth of the largest fortune, away from both the crowd of ordinary agents and the few richest; it gives a slope of about −1.2 to −1.35 (125 × 125 town, three runs each with n = 0.2, 1 and 5). You can see the present round, or everything since you last changed a setting (its first third left out, so the town has time to settle).

Agents, on the left: P(τ), how many rounds an agent lasts between two bankruptcies. In the standard setup the self-organized town has none — without upkeep nobody's money can fall below zero — and the plot says so; when the buyers pay, agents go bankrupt when their shopping costs more than they have. With upkeep (the μ buttons) nearly all bankruptcies are failed B factories, at the end of a cycle like this one. Right after a bankruptcy an agent has only its wage, 0.05 a round. After 40 rounds it can afford a bakery (2). It goes on saving until it can try a B factory (8) — but with μ = 0.02 a B factory costs 0.16 a round in upkeep, more than three wages, and if it does not sell well the agent is soon bankrupt again. On the 125 × 125 town that takes half the time more than 320 rounds; a quarter of the cycles are shorter than 180 rounds, and a quarter longer than 490. The decades slider over this plot shows fewer decades of the y axis, to look closely at the common times. On the right: the money of single agents, round by round. The plot follows the 10, 5 or 3 richest agents, or agents picked at random. The round button over the plot switches between 10 largest, 5 largest, 10 at random and 5 at random; the buttons over the section let you choose freely, also 3. Each agent is drawn for its whole life: from its last bankruptcy (or the start of the run) until it goes bankrupt again, where a cross marks the end. One of the richest is also let go once its fortune is gone — its money back to the mean money of the town — where a circle marks the end. Then the richest agent not yet shown (or another one picked at random) takes its place, drawn back to where its own life began (after a very long life, as far back as it is kept — normally at least to the last time it had nothing). The slider sets the time window: from now back in time, each step 1.5 times longer, and at its right end the whole run. The colour of the line shows what the agent was making at the time; grey means no factory, or one opened that very round. In the standard setup a rich agent's money climbs while it owns a big company, then drains away in grey stretches while it tries one new factory after another. With patience 4 the richest keep their companies, and their money climbs steadily.

A life ends: bankrupt or money 0. These buttons over the section give both plots another view. With money 0 a life ends whenever the agent's money falls to zero — to one wage or less — whether it goes bankrupt or spends its last savings on a new factory, and the fortunes plot follows each of the richest from the last time it had nothing until its money is all gone. With upkeep 0.02 two thirds of these lives last 40 rounds: from nothing an agent saves its wage, 0.05 a round, for 40 rounds until it can buy a bakery (2), and buying it leaves it with nothing again. The standard setup has no upkeep, and then nobody goes bankrupt in the self-organized economy — money comes in as wage and sales, and goes out only when an agent buys a factory with money it has — so P(τ) of the bankruptcies stays empty. Lives that end at money 0 remain: an agent whose factories fail saves 40 rounds for a bakery, then 160 rounds for a B factory (8), and so on, and P(τ) has peaks at 40 and 160 rounds (about 41 % and 33 % of the lives).

Around the high-tech companies: the mean and the median money of the agents, and the mean size of the bread factories and of the ordinary (B) factories, against the distance d to the nearest big company of the most scalable product (one that sells at least a tenth of what the biggest one sells), summed over the last 100 rounds or over the last two thirds of the run. Linear, log–lin (only the vertical axis logarithmic) or log–log. Close to a big chip company the mean money is high — its owner lives there — and the bakeries sell many times more than far away (about 7 times in the standard setup, 10 to 20 times with n = 0.2 and upkeep) — most likely because the rich order more, and bread is bought close by. Some ten cells away both curves are flat. The median changes much less than the mean: the wealth sits with a few, not with the whole neighbourhood.

Lessons from the model

Each lesson below was checked by running the model on a 125 × 125 town, three runs of 1000 rounds each, measured over the last 700. Lessons 1 to 7 used the earlier standard setup — the self-organized price, n = 0.2, patience 1 and upkeep 0.02 — unless said otherwise; lesson 8 the present one: n = 1, patience 0 and no upkeep. Scaling means the slopes of the straight lines in the log–log plots: P(s) ∝ s−2 for the bakeries (with n = 0.2), and the tail of P(M), about M−1.24.

  1. Making the high-tech factories dearer to start does not change the scaling. With B and C half as dear, or two or four times as dear (up to 32 and 80 instead of 8 and 20), the bakeries keep P(s) ∝ s−2.0 to s−2.1, and the money tail stays between −1.06 and −1.28, with no trend. What changes is how much happens. Four times dearer gives about 5 times fewer start-ups (30 instead of 138 a round), 10 times fewer bankruptcies, almost 4 times fewer chip factories, and three times more money in the agents' pockets (mean 13.8 instead of 4.6): the thermostat needs more savings before anybody can afford to try.
  2. More upkeep does not change the scaling of the companies, but it can wipe out the bakeries. From μ = 0.01 to 0.08 the bakeries keep P(s) ∝ s−2.0, while the money tail becomes a little flatter, from −1.36 to −1.10. The big change comes at a threshold: once a bakery's upkeep, μ · 2, is more than the wage, 0.05, a bakery that sells nothing loses money every round. At μ = 0.04 the bakeries drop from 14 400 to 1 500, bankruptcies rise from 25 to 360 a round, and an agent lasts just 40 rounds between two bankruptcies — exactly the time it takes to save up for a bakery, which then sinks it.
  3. When the buyers pay, money gets scarce, bread production collapses, and markets concentrate. The mean money falls from 4.6 to 1.5 and the typical money (the median) from 3.2 to 0.8, while the richest still hold about 1 700. Bankruptcies rise from 25 to 360 a round, and half the time an agent goes bankrupt again within 6 rounds: mostly agents without a factory, whose shopping, delivery included, costs more than their wage. There are 17 times fewer bakeries (830 instead of 14 400), and the biggest chip company's share of its market almost doubles, from 0.21 to 0.39. The scaling of the bakeries changes little, s−1.9 instead of s−2.1. Transport takes about 410 money out of the town every round. Without upkeep, as in the present standard setup, the bread survives — 10 300 bakeries instead of 12 400, with n = 1 — but the chip market still concentrates, from 0.23 to 0.40 (lesson 8).
  4. More patience makes the rich richer, not the typical agent. With patience 3 instead of 1, the richest agent holds about 6 900 instead of 1 800 and the mean money rises from 4.6 to 11.5, but the median stays at 3.2–3.4. The money tail flattens from M−1.24 to M−0.87: owners of big companies keep them longer and pile up money, while everybody else lives as before. (On a 64 × 64 town the leading chip company lasted about 8 rounds instead of 1 to 2.)
  5. A protected country in the middle gets richer — in both economies. Put a circle with 10 % of the town in the middle, with a tariff of 1 on goods going in. One might expect the insiders to get poor when the buyers pay, since they pay the tariff on what they import. That does not happen. With the self-organized price the insiders end up with 1.6 times as much money as the outsiders, and when the buyers pay with 5 times as much (3 to 7 in the three runs). Their income per round is 1.7 and 2.7 times that of the outsiders, in all three runs, against 0.7 to 2.2 times in the same circle without a border, where it only depends on where the giants happen to sit. The reason: behind the border the insiders buy practically everything at home, so they hardly pay any tariff. Their own B and C companies grow — the inside holds 10 % of the people but about a quarter (self-organized) to 40 % (buyers pay) of all the chip factories — and they sell to the outside for free.
  6. A 100 % tax on goods going in does the same. With the same circle and a tax of 100 % instead of the tariff, the insiders end up with 1.8 times as much money as the outsiders with the self-organized price, and 7.6 times as much when the buyers pay; their income is 1.7 and 3.4 times higher, in all three runs. Again they buy nearly everything at home, from their own chip makers — about 6 of the town's 28 sit inside.
  7. A middle product gives failed investments a soft landing. Keep only A and C (with C at γ = −0.9 or −1 alike): big chip companies still last about four rounds, but every agent that has saved up 20 now tries a chip factory — 41 tries a round instead of 17 — and only 7 % of them grow big instead of 14 %. With B the savers try B at 8 instead, so a failed try costs 8, not 20, and 9 % of those tries last ten rounds, against 0.2 % of the chip tries. The owners of a big chip company that ends try some 17 new ones over the next 80 rounds in both towns, but with B they keep most of their fortune (median about 300 → 260), while without B it is gone within 40 rounds (220 → 20). So without the middle no fortune lasts: the mean money, 10.0, is the median, against 4.6 and 3.2 with B. (When the buyers pay, with n = 5, the chip companies also last about four rounds and the mean comes close to the median without B, but whether the owners keep their fortunes varies from run to run.)
  8. Patience and upkeep are zero in the standard setup, and the town is then steady. Over 4 000 rounds about 1 % of the agents have no money (one wage or less), and the poorer half of the town keeps about 16 % of all the money, in both economies and without a trend. (In real countries the poorer half owns 5 to 10 % of all wealth [11].) Patience up to 3 leaves the chip giants at about four rounds; 4 and 5 are another world (see Patience). Upkeep, the μ buttons, removes the factories that sell nothing — without it 97 % of the bakeries and B factories sell nothing in a given round. In the self-organized economy that changes the details more than the picture: of 3 150 B factories, 200 are left at μ = 0.02, and owners of failing B factories now go bankrupt, 25 to 50 a round. A threshold comes at μ = 0.03, when a bakery's upkeep, 2μ, is more than the wage (lesson 2): the bakeries drop from 14 400 to 2 000, the median money from about 3 to 1, and the richest 1 % own 58 % of the money. When the buyers pay, μ = 0.01 is already a big step, because the shopping takes about three quarters of a wage: the bakeries drop from 10 300 to 2 500 (280 at μ = 0.02), and the share of agents with no money rises from 1 % to 5 % (9 % at 0.02, 27 % at 0.05). In no run does that share grow in time. But with upkeep the money slowly piles up at the top — the mean keeps rising while the median stays — and the poorer half's share keeps falling: with μ = 0.02 from 17 % to 10 % between rounds 1 000 and 4 000 in the self-organized economy, and to 2–6 % when the buyers pay.

Try this

  • Let the town run a few hundred rounds, then set patience to 5: the richest get much richer, and now and then a chip giant rules for a hundred rounds or more. Then press ↺ for a new town with patience 5: it freezes.
  • Choose the Globalization preset in the control panel: transport gets cheaper every round, and local producers merge into a few big ones.
  • Turn on markets C, then draw a border with a 100 % tax right through the biggest C market. Remove it (− border) and draw one with a tariff of 0.5 instead.
  • Draw a road across the town and see which products grow along it.
  • Switch the economy to buyers pay and compare the money columns. Then click μ .02 and watch the bakeries disappear and the money gather around the chip giants.

Related models

The model sits where economics and physics meet, and older models share parts of it.

Shops on a map. Hotelling [2] was the first to put sellers on a map: customers along a street buy from the shop whose price plus walking cost is lowest. The rule here, C = sγ + β·d, is the same idea, in two dimensions and with a price that falls as the factory grows.

Economic geography. In Krugman's model [3] economies of scale and transport costs decide whether industry spreads out or gathers in one region, and when transport gets cheap enough, manufacturing concentrates. The town here reaches the same conclusion, but from simple agents that only react to their own profits, without the equilibrium and foresight of the economic models — and it shows the order: the products with the strongest economy of scale and the cheapest transport go global first.

Lock-in. Arthur [4] showed that with increasing returns, small chance events decide which technology wins, and the winner is then hard to dislodge. A chip giant here is such a lock-in in space: inside its reach R no newcomer can win a customer. The original model [1] also finds that new companies tend to reappear close to old ones: history matters.

Trade networks. The closest recent model we found is by Han, Schröder, Witthaut and Böttcher [5]: agents choose whom to trade with, with economies of scale, transport costs and different tastes. They find three regimes — centralized production with a star-shaped trade network, distributed production where everybody trades with everybody, and local production without trade — much like the chips, the ordinary industry and the bread here.

Avalanches. Bak, Chen, Scheinkman and Woodford [6] showed that producers who interact locally, with economies of scale, can turn small independent shocks into large swings of the whole economy (self-organized criticality). The steady churn of start-ups, giants and crashes here is of the same kind.

Money. When money is only passed around, never created or destroyed, it spreads like energy in a gas, with an exponential distribution, as Dragulescu and Yakovenko showed [7]. When wealth also grows by risky investments, the rich form a power-law (Pareto) tail, as in the model of Bouchaud and Mézard [8]. In this town money is created (wages and sales) and destroyed (start costs and upkeep), and P(M) has a power-law tail, falling as about M−1.2 to M−1.35 per unit of money. Real wealth tails are usually steeper.

Company sizes. Axtell found [9] that the sizes of US companies follow Zipf's law: the number of companies bigger than s falls as 1/s, so the density falls as s−2. The bread companies here come close with n = 0.2: their fitted slope is about −2 (−1.9 to −2.2 in our runs). With more orders per agent it is flatter, about −1.5 with n = 1 and −1.2 with n = 5, because s = 1 + (units sold) counts units, not customers: with n = 1 a bakery reaches the bend of its economy of scale with one customer, with n = 0.2 it needs five. Stanley and co-workers [10] found that big companies grow and shrink less, in proportion, than small ones; one could test that in this town too.

In a short search of the literature we found no other model that puts these pieces together — many products, each with its own economy of scale and transport cost, on a map, with prices that set themselves — beyond the one-product original [1].
