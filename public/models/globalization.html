Globalization in a nutshell — 2D

Why is bread still baked around the corner, while almost every computer chip in the world comes from a handful of factories? This applet is a toy world for thinking about that question. It is the model of Sneppen and Bornholdt [1], made two-dimensional and — the real change — extended from one product to many. With many products, agents choose what to make, and that needs a careful account of what holds back a big economy of scale: it costs money to start a factory, and money every round to keep one running. Without those costs, the products that scale best would simply take over everything.

The world

Picture a square town of L × L people, the agents, one on each square of a grid. The town has edges, like an island (closed boundaries, the default); with periodic boundaries the grid is wrapped around like a doughnut instead, so there is no edge: walk off the right side and you come back on the left. Everybody needs N kinds of goods, called A, B, C, … Some agents also own a factory that makes one of them. An agent has money, and a list of the products it can make: F(r,i) = 1 if the agent at r makes product i, otherwise 0.

What a thing costs

Suppose you live at r₂ and want one unit of product i. Each factory offers it to you at a price, and you simply take the cheapest offer. The factory at r₁ asks

C = s(r₁,i)γ(i) + β(i) · |r₁ − r₂|   ( with roads and borders: see below )

The first term is what it costs to make the thing. Here s is the size of the factory: one plus the number of units it sold last round. A brand-new factory has s = 1, so its price is 1. The exponent γ is negative, and it says how much being big helps. With γ = −0.3 a factory a hundred times bigger makes each unit only about four times as cheaply — that is bread. With γ = −0.9 it makes each unit sixty times as cheaply — that is a computer chip. This is the economy of scale.

The second term is the cost of transport: every step between factory and customer adds β, and distance is measured in a straight line, the short way round. Nobody buys from themselves: you shop at somebody else's factory.

You can change the geography. A road makes travel along it cheaper: the transport cost there is multiplied by a factor you choose, say 0.2. A border charges a tax on goods that cross it, a percentage of what they are worth at that point (their price plus the transport so far); a one-way border taxes goods in one direction only. Its arrows show which way: goods crossing the border in the direction the arrows point pay the tax, goods crossing the other way pass free. (The arrows point to your left as you trace the border from where you started drawing to where you let go: draw it from top to bottom and they point to the right.) Goods always take the cheapest way: they may follow a road for part of the way and then leave it, and if a border cuts only across a road, they may leave the road and walk around the border's end rather than pay the tax. With roads or borders the cheapest offer is no longer the one on the straight line, so the applet finds it the way water finds its way downhill: it spreads every factory's offer step by step over the grid (along 16 directions), always keeping the cheapest offer that has reached each place. (For speed it also works this way when a product has very many factories; the distances then differ from straight lines by at most about 3 %.)

So each product has just three numbers: Start, the price of building a factory for it; γ; and β. It is natural that the products that profit most from being big are also the ones that cost most to start: the applet insists on that ordering. Product A is the basic one — cheap to start (2), helped only a little by size (γ = −0.3), and costly to carry around (β = 0.1).

One round, step by step

  1. Shopping. For each product, n·L² orders are placed. Who places them? Agents picked at random — but an agent that took in more money last round is picked more often. The rich shop more, and that helps their neighbours.
  2. Selling. Each order goes to the cheapest offer (a tie is settled by a coin toss). The sizes, and so the prices, stay fixed during the round, so you buy each product from the same factory all round long.
  3. Bookkeeping. After the round, each agent's money changes by ΔM = w + p · (units sold) − μ · Σits factories Start Everybody earns the same wage w; it is how a person without a business can save up to start one. Each unit sold brings in the price p. And every factory costs a little upkeep each round, a fraction μ of what it cost to build: machines wear out, so a factory must keep selling to pay for itself.
    What is the price p? In the self-organized economy (the default; the button in the upper right corner cycles through self-organized with n = 0.2 orders per agent and product, self-organized with n = 5, and the fixed price) it adjusts itself, so that everything sold is worth exactly what everybody earned in wages: p(next round) = Σ wages / Σ units sold (this round) If the world makes twice as much, each unit is worth half as much. That is why money cannot pile up without end. With the fixed price (button off) every unit earns 1, as in the original model, and a successful company grows rich without limit.
    An agent whose money falls below zero is bankrupt: its least profitable factory closes, the debt is forgiven, and it starts again from money w (from 0 with the fixed price).
  4. Deciding. Each agent compares this round's profit (p · units sold − upkeep) with last round's. Things going fine? Then it carries on. But if the profit fell by more than ordinary luck would explain — more than κ·p·√(units sold), the typical size of the random ups and downs of a count — or if there is no profit, or no factory at all, the agent tries something new. It picks at random a product it does not make yet and can afford, pays Start, and opens that factory, which begins small (s = 1). Nobody may make more than lim products; at the limit the agent closes its weakest factory to make room, so with lim = 1 it changes trade.

At the start, every agent makes one product picked at random and has money M₀. That is all there is to the model.

What to look for

The key question is: can a newcomer ever beat a giant? The newcomer sells at price 1, the giant at sγ, which may be very small. A customer at distance d from the giant goes to the newcomer only if the giant's transport cost has eaten up its advantage. So the giant keeps every customer within a distance of about R ≈ (1 − sγ)/β. If R is bigger than the whole world, no newcomer can win even one customer, anywhere. The giant is then a natural world monopoly, and it falls only if it gives up by itself.

Now you can see what globalization does. Make transport cheaper (the transport factor multiplies every β; lower gradually keeps reducing it, as in Fig. 2 of [1]) and R grows: many small local producers merge into a few big ones that ship far. Products with a strong economy of scale and cheap transport go global first; bread stays local. A road stretches R along itself, so companies grow along roads; a border with a tax cuts R down again, and each country gets its own champion — unless the economy of scale is so strong that the giant across the border is still cheaper after the tax.

How to read the pictures

3D: the town seen from the side, as in the figures of [1]. Every factory is a tower as tall as the units it sold last round; an agent with several products has its towers stacked, the biggest at the bottom. The scale bars at the left corner tell you the heights: units sold upward (one round number on the linear scale, the powers of ten on the log scale), and money. The agents' money can be shown as golden glass towers, either around the factories or hanging below the ground. The height scales are the average size of the biggest company over the last two thirds of the run, and they are renewed only each time the run has grown by half, so the picture keeps still. Drag to turn and tilt, turn the mouse wheel to zoom, or use the view menu in the upper left corner, which also sets the perspective and the zoom. When a company collapses, its tower sinks to zero over one round.

Agents: everyone who sold something is a pie, its area the units sold, a slice per product. Market: for one product, everybody in the colour of the factory they buy from; the dots are the factories. Sales and Money: heat maps on a log scale, in the colours of the paper's figures. Hover over or tap an agent to see its numbers.

The buttons in the upper left corner draw the geography: + road (its ▾ sets the transport factor on the road) and + border (its ▾ sets the tax and whether it is one-way): choose one, then press and drag on the lattice — the road or border follows your finger, so it can bend and curve — and release. − road and − border: choose one, then tap a road or border to remove it. Tap the chosen button again to go back to turning the view. ✕ all removes every road and border.

The buttons in the lower right corner: the lightning bolt makes a small explosion wherever a company crashes — either it goes bankrupt (only small ones do), or it closes because its owner gives it up for another product when the profit falls (that is how big companies end: they have money enough never to go bankrupt). The bigger the company, the bigger the bang: one blast on top of its tower, of radius growing as the cube root of its size, and small lightnings along its golden fortune pillar. The many failed start-ups give only small bursts, at most 60 at a time — off (black), yellow, or in the company's colour (multicoloured bolt); the half-black circle switches the background; .png saves the picture with the parameters written in the lower left corner; sq saves just what is drawn, cut tight.

Below the town: how the numbers change in time, and two log–log plots — how the money is spread over the agents, and how big the companies of each product are. The dashed lines are power-law fits: for money, over its tail; for companies, over the product whose companies are smallest on average. Either plot shows the present, or the sum over the last two thirds of the run since you last changed a parameter. Each plot has its own .png and sq buttons: a picture in the shape shown, or a square one, both with title and axis labels.
