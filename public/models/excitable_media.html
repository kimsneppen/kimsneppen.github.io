Discrete Excitable Media

0 → 1 → … → Max → f → 0▾

An excitable medium is a spatially extended system whose elements sit in a stable resting state, can be pushed into a short excited state by an excited neighbour, and then pass through a refractory period during which they cannot fire again. Excitation therefore travels as a wave that cannot turn back into the tissue it has just left. Heart muscle, nerve fibres, the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction and aggregating Dictyostelium amoebae are classic examples. Their typical patterns are target waves around pacemakers and rotating spirals, which become scroll waves in 3D.

First model

Norbert Wiener and Arturo Rosenblueth gave the first mathematical model of an excitable medium while studying impulse conduction in heart muscle. Each element is resting, excited or refractory, and they showed how a wave circulating around an obstacle can sustain flutter by re-entry.

N. Wiener & A. Rosenblueth, Arch. Inst. Cardiol. México 16, 205 (1946).

First cellular automaton

James Greenberg and Stuart Hastings turned the idea into a cellular automaton on a lattice: a resting site becomes excited if a neighbour is excited, then steps through a fixed sequence of refractory states back to rest. The model in this applet is a stochastic variant: the refractory count 0…Max advances with probability p, Max is the resting excitable state and f is the excited state.

J. M. Greenberg & S. P. Hastings, SIAM J. Appl. Math. 34, 515 (1978).

Defects and atrial fibrillation

Christensen, Manani and Peters modelled atrial tissue as a lattice of cells in which neighbouring cells along the fibre direction are always coupled, while side-to-side (transverse) couplings exist only with some probability. As transverse couplings become sparse, small circuits of poorly coupled cells can trap a re-entrant wave, and a regular paced rhythm switches to fibrillation-like turbulence. The model identifies critical regions whose ablation stops the fibrillation.

K. Christensen, K. A. Manani & N. S. Peters, Simple model for identifying critical regions in atrial fibrillation, Phys. Rev. Lett. 114, 028104 (2015).

In the applet, pcut removes this fraction of the nearest-neighbour connections at random. Set Cut to "transverse only" to keep the connections along x intact, as in that model (pcut is then one minus the transverse coupling probability). Try a square lattice with the pacemaker on and raise pcut towards 1. pdefect instead removes whole sites by holding them at 0.

Demonstrations

The three buttons under the title start ready-made runs. Click a running demo again to flip its parameters; the lattice state is kept when you flip back, so you can see that fibrillation, once started, can persist (it is metastable). Real ⇄ Sick: anatomical FCC heart with Max = 100; Sick restarts the heart with pcut = 0.83 of all connections cut (if no wave gets through, a new random cut pattern is drawn until one does), and Real restores all connections without resetting. 2D ⇄ 2D-stoc and 1D ⇄ 1D p=0.9 start from the default settings (Max = 12, no defects, sources or cuts, black background), with p = 1 or 0.92 in 2D and p = 1 or 0.9 in 1D.

Heart geometry

The Heart view runs the same automaton on the outer H cell layers of a heart-shaped solid whose largest horizontal circumference is L sites, on a simple cubic or FCC lattice. Click the button again to switch to Real H., a rough anatomical shape with tapered ventricles, two atria, the aorta, the pulmonary trunk and the superior vena cava. The great vessels are open-ended hollow tubes that contain no heart muscle and stay inactive. They are coloured as in anatomy atlases: red for oxygenated blood (the four pulmonary veins coming from the lungs, and the aorta), blue for deoxygenated blood (the pulmonary arteries going to the lungs, and the superior vena cava). The exception is the myocardial sleeves: atrial muscle extends a short way into each pulmonary vein and into the superior vena cava, and these sleeves are excitable. Pulmonary-vein sleeves are the most common source of the ectopic beats that trigger atrial fibrillation. The pacemaker sits where the sinus node would be: the top of one lobe, or the junction of the superior vena cava and the right atrium. Cut the heart open with zc to see the wall.

Model

Each site holds s ∈ {0,…,Max, f}. Per step: s<Max → s+1 with probability p; Max → f only if a nearest neighbour is f (or a source fires); f → 0. Defects are held at 0; a cut connection never transmits excitation. 1D: open ends. 2D: periodic in L and H; square (4 neighbours, periodic) or triangular (6 neighbours, open boundaries, drawn as a rhombus). 3D: simple cubic (6 neighbours, periodic in both L directions) or face-centred cubic (12 neighbours, stacked triangular layers, open boundaries); open top and bottom along H in both. psource sets the density of randomly placed pacemaker sites, which fire whenever they recover to Max; pspont is an optional per-step probability that any resting site fires on its own. Defects are added or removed one by one as pdefect changes; slide to 0 and back for a fresh pattern. Click or drag on the 1D/2D lattice to excite sites; drag the 3D view to rotate.
