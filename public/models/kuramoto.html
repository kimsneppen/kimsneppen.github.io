Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation

This is the simplest interface equation with an intrinsic instability. Such an instability occurs whenever the parts of an interface that are ahead tend to propagate faster than the parts that lag behind. A concrete example is the fire front of a burning sheet of paper: the sections of the front that are ahead face the most unburned material, and therefore advance the fastest. To lowest order this self-amplification is the linear instability

∂h/∂t = −∇²h,

a negative diffusion under which an evolving interface grows at all wavelengths — and the shorter the wavelength, the faster the growth, without bound. A physical front must have a short-wavelength cut-off (for a flame, e.g. a minimal ignition heat), supplied by the gentlest possible regularization, −∇⁴h. Adding also the simplest possible nonlinearity, the tilt-dependent growth (∇h)², one obtains the deterministic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (KS) equation

∂h/∂t = −∇²h − ∇⁴h + (∇h)²,

where the constants in front of the three terms have been rescaled away by an appropriate scaling of space x, time t and height h. The competition between long-wavelength growth, short-wavelength damping and nonlinear mode coupling produces cells of a preferred size (λ = 2π√2 in these units) in a state of perpetual spatiotemporal chaos.

Chaos as noise: the KPZ connection. The KS equation shares the basic symmetries of the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation, and its spatiotemporal chaos acts on large scales as an emergent, self-generated noise. One therefore expects the large-scale behaviour of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation to fall in the same universality class as the KPZ equation, as explored in Refs. [3–5]. In this translation the characteristic cell has wavelength 2π/kmax, where kmax = 1/√2 is the wavenumber selected by the linearly unstable term, and the emergent large-scale dynamics carries an effective diffusion constant of about 15 (see Refs. [3–5]).

Variant models: extra terms in the equation

The KS equation is the lowest-order description of a growing front: it keeps only the first terms of two expansions — in derivatives (long wavelengths) and in the slope ∇h (gentle tilts). The header lets you go one step further in either expansion and see what survives.

Higher derivatives

The toggle at the left replaces the two linear terms by +∇⁴h + ∇⁶h. The instability is then carried by the fourth derivative and the cut-off by the sixth; a wave with wavenumber k grows at the rate ω = k⁴ − k⁶ instead of k² − k⁴. The unstable band 0 < k < 1 is the same, but the fastest mode moves from k = 1/√2 to k = √(2/3) and the growth near k = 0 is much weaker. The chaotic cell pattern persists with slightly smaller cells — a first hint that the qualitative picture does not depend on the exact form of the linear terms.

The slope expansion: α and β

A front that advances at a speed depending only on its local tilt, v(∇h), adds a term v(∇h) to ∂h/∂t. Expanding v in the slope gives, by the symmetry x → −x, only even powers and even functions: |∇h|, (∇h)², (∇h)⁴, … The KS equation keeps (∇h)², the term that lets a tilted piece of front grow faster (a tilted flame front sees more fuel per unit length). The two pull-down menus switch on its neighbours:

α|∇h| is the term below (∇h)² in the expansion — it is not analytic at zero slope and describes a front whose speed changes at first order in the tilt, like a facetted crystal. With α > 0 tilted parts grow faster still: the cells become flatter and sharper, ridges form facets, the surface width W shrinks (W ≈ 0.8 at α = 1 against 2.7 for KS on the default lattice) while the exponent stays chaotic, λ ≈ 0.09. With α < 0 tilted parts are slowed: the cells grow taller and rounder (W ≈ 4.7 at α = −1), and the dynamics remains chaotic, λ ≈ 0.08.

β(∇h)⁴ is the term above (∇h)². It is small where slopes are gentle and takes over where they are steep — precisely the place where the long-wavelength, small-slope expansion behind KS is least trustworthy.

The sign of the correction decides

Since (∇h)² ≥ 0 always adds growth, the sign of the next term decides whether steep slopes are rewarded further or held back, and the two signs give genuinely different worlds (measured on the default lattice, L = 128, periodic, after a long run):

β > 0 rewards steep regions with extra growth. The steep flanks race ahead, the surface flattens (W drops from 2.7 to 1.3 at β = 2) and the mean height climbs faster. At β = 0.5 the front is still clearly chaotic (λ ≈ 0.07; the shape changes by more than its own width every 20 time units). By β = 2 the chaos is essentially gone: the front settles into a steady cellular pattern that changes by only a tenth of its width in 20 time units, and the measured exponent drops to λ ≈ 0.02 — a growth time of 50 time units, an order of magnitude slower than the cell dynamics of KS. What remains is metastability, not chaos: long steady episodes that are very weakly unstable, ending now and then in a rearrangement of a few cells, after which a new steady front forms. With (∇h)² switched off and β(∇h)⁴ as the only nonlinearity the front is steady outright, and the long-time divergence of two nearly identical fronts is nil (free run slope ≈ 0.00), even though the Benettin average taken over a whole episode may show a small positive number (see Benettin versus free run below).

β < 0 penalizes steep slopes. The cells grow tall and the front freezes: already at β = −0.5 the largest Lyapunov exponent drops to zero (measured λ = −0.001), and at β = −1.5 it is 0.01 — a steady or slowly drifting cellular pattern instead of spatiotemporal chaos. The Lyapunov vector then has a very long wavelength (k̄ ≈ 0.16 rather than the cell wavenumber 0.7): what remains "unstable" is a slow, large-scale reshuffling of cells, not the splitting and merging that defines KS chaos.

The full λ(β) dependence for both equations, measured after the transient, is tabulated at the end of this text. This is the sobering lesson of the variant models. The chaos of the KS equation is robust against the |∇h| correction, but a (∇h)⁴ term of moderate size — of either sign, once |β| is of order one — eliminates it: negative β freezes the cells, positive β flattens them into a steady pattern with only rare rearrangements. And it is not even robust against the form of the linear terms in this respect: with +∇⁴h + ∇⁶h in place of −∇²h − ∇⁴h the chaos survives every positive β (table). Whether a real front is chaotic therefore depends on physics that the lowest-order expansion has thrown away — which casts doubt on the range of validity of the long-wavelength, small-slope approximation as a description of real chaotic fronts, and suggests that the universal large-scale (KPZ) behaviour should be looked for only where the slopes stay gentle. The term budget of the Lyapunov section (below) shows the mechanism directly: switch β on and watch the β(∇h)⁴ strip take away the divergence that (∇h)² and −∇²h produce on the steep flanks.

Two more terms with second derivatives: γ and δ

The last two menus add terms of the next order in the derivative expansion that are still second order in the slope. γ∇²(∇h)² is the conserved counterpart of the KS nonlinearity: being a Laplacian of something, it moves height around without changing the mean — the nonlinearity of conserved growth (Villain–Lai–Das Sarma). δ∇²h·(∇h)² is a diffusion whose coefficient is the squared slope: for δ > 0 steep parts of the front are smoothed harder than flat parts, for δ < 0 they are sharpened. Both terms carry two more derivatives than (∇h)², and that decides their fate: for the "wrong" sign they are not regularized by −∇⁴h at all. γ > 0 and δ < 0 blow up in finite time — at every time step tried, from 0.02 down to 0.002, so this is the equation and not the integrator — and the run then stops with the "diverged" warning; the δ menu therefore offers only δ ≥ 0. For γ < 0 and δ > 0 the runs are bounded and the integration is converged already at the automatic time step (γ: 0.02, δ: 0.01; W agrees with a dt = 0.002 reference to 0.1 % for |γ| ≤ 5 and at dt = 0.01 for δ). A negative γ flattens the KS pattern strongly (W ≈ 0.9 at γ = −0.5, 0.6 at −2) while the mean height still climbs; a positive δ keeps W near the KS value (1.1–2.2). Both appear in the Lyapunov term budget and in the colour menu like the other terms, so their share of the divergence can be read off directly — at γ = −0.5 in 2D, for instance, the γ term adds +0.18 to Λ while at δ = 0.5 the δ term removes −0.14, in both cases with the total still near the KS value.

Practical notes: the (∇h)² term itself can be crossed out in the header. The equation is then linear and would grow without bound, so β jumps to 1 automatically (and back to 0 when the term returns) — a way to see that β(∇h)⁴ alone can saturate the instability, giving cells of the same size but a different shape. The time step follows the menus automatically, because the fourth power of the slope needs a finer step than KS.

How chaotic? The largest Lyapunov exponent

The idea

Chaos means that two states that start almost identical end up completely different. How fast? Take the running surface h and a twin that differs from it by a tiny random displacement δh of root-mean-square size ε = 10−6, and integrate both with the very same equation. If the state is chaotic the separation grows exponentially, |δh| ∝ eλt, and the growth rate λ is the largest Lyapunov exponent: positive means the pattern cannot be predicted beyond a time of order 1/λ; zero or negative means the pattern is periodic or steady. For the KS equation on a large box λ ≈ 0.09 in the units of the equation — a memory of about ten time units, roughly the time a cell takes to split. Open the section below the display to start the measurement.

Benettin's method

An exponentially growing separation soon stops being small, so the applet measures it in short pieces. After every frame it records how far the twin has drifted, |δh|, adds ln(|δh|/ε) to a running total, and pulls the twin back to distance ε along the same direction. The total divided by the elapsed time is the growth rate, and λ is its time average. The applet averages only over the last two thirds of the time since the last reset or change of the equation (the window is shown in the panel head): the first third, in which the twin still turns into the Lyapunov vector and a freshly changed equation still settles, is dropped. The same window is used for the term shares below. Right after a reset of the whole surface the separation grows at the fastest linear rate, ωmax = 1/4, and λ drops from there as the chaos saturates; the ↻ button restarts the twin and the average. (The random seed is smoothed to the unstable band and the first frame is not counted, so that the short waves the −∇⁴ term wipes out in a single step do not bias the average.)

The Lyapunov vector: where the unpredictability sits

Because the twin is always pulled back along its present direction, that direction δh(x) converges to the Lyapunov vector: the perturbation that grows fastest in the present state. The panel draws it. It is not spread evenly over the surface but localized on the one or two cells that are just about to split or merge — this is where the future of the pattern is decided. The red marker sits on the most unstable node (where |δh| peaks), λloc is the growth rate of this particular vector right now (averaged over the last two time units), and k̄ is its rms wavenumber, close to the cell wavenumber 1/√2 in KS chaos.

Free run: the long-time check

The free run switch never pulls the twin back. The panel then plots the distance |δh| between the two interfaces on a logarithmic scale against time: a straight line means the separation grows as eλt all the way up to the dashed level √2·W, the distance of two completely diverged, uncorrelated interfaces, where the curve must bend over and saturate. λ is read off as the fitted slope of the straight stretch, and |δh|/√2W tells how far the divergence has come (1 = complete). From ε = 10−6 with λ ≈ 0.09 this takes about ln(106)/λ ≈ 150 time units.

Benettin versus free run — and can the separation shrink?

In principle the two measure the same thing: the sum of the frame-by-frame logarithms in Benettin's method is the logarithm of the free separation, as long as the separation stays small, and the applet checks this for you — the exact term budget (next section) reproduces the Benettin growth of every single frame to four digits. When the two numbers nevertheless differ, look at the window and at the state. For a chaotic KS front they agree within a few per cent after a hundred time units. For a front that is steady or nearly so — large |β|, or (∇h)⁴ as the only nonlinearity — the picture changes: the state is metastable. A steady cellular front can be very weakly unstable (growth rate a few hundredths) for hundreds of time units and then rearrange a few cells, after which a new steady front with a different stability forms. A Benettin average therefore depends on which episodes it covers, and a random seed first loses its damped components and can be transiently amplified before it settles into the slowest direction. The free run shows all of this as the shape of the |δh|(t) curve: a dip, a hump, straight stretches with different slopes. For such states trust the free-run slope over the most recent straight stretch, and read a small Benettin value as "not chaotic in any practical sense" — a growth time of 50 or 100 time units on a front that looks frozen is a slow instability of one particular configuration, not spatiotemporal chaos.

And yes, the separation can shrink. If the front is linearly stable the largest exponent is negative and |δh| decays exponentially — the free-run curve is a straight line going down, until it reaches the round-off floor at about 10−16 of the surface height, where it flattens. The fit then reports the negative slope over the last two thirds of the record. A state with λ = 0 exactly — a steady front perturbed along its neutral translation mode, for periodic boundaries — shows a flat line. In Benettin mode a negative λ shows directly as a negative running average.

Which term produces the chaos? The term budget

An exact bookkeeping

λ tells how fast two nearly identical fronts separate; the budget tells which term of the equation is responsible, and where. Write the equation as ∂h/∂t = Σi Ti(h), one Ti per term. The twin obeys the same equation, so the difference δh = twin − field obeys exactly

∂tδh = Σi [Ti(twin) − Ti(field)].

For a linear term the bracket is simply Ti(δh); for a nonlinear term it is the exact difference (no linearization is made, so the identity holds at any separation). Multiply by δh and average over the box: since d/dt ½⟨δh²⟩ = ⟨δh·∂tδh⟩, the growth rate of the separation splits into one share per term,

Λ = d ln|δh|/dt = Σi Λi,   Λi = ⟨ci⟩,   ci(x) = δh(x)·[Ti(twin) − Ti(field)](x) / ⟨δh²⟩.

ci(x) is the local contribution of term i to the divergence rate, in the units of λ: positive where that term drives the two fronts apart, negative where it pulls them together. Its spatial mean Λi is the term's share of the instantaneous exponent, and the time average of ΣiΛi must be λ itself. The panel shows this sum as Σ terms next to the Benettin value; the two agree to three digits once the average has run for a while, which checks both measurements at once.

Reading the plots

The terms button (Benettin mode) opens, under the vector, one strip per term of the equation: ci(x) with the lobes that add to the divergence in red and those that remove from it in blue. The strips have their own scales, because the terms differ by orders of magnitude; each caption gives the term's time-averaged and present share. The last strip is the total λ(x) = Σici(x). Below it a stacked plot on the same scale as that strip builds the total up from the most dissipative term: each shaded band, in the term's colour, is what the next term adds above or removes below the running sum, and the full line on top is λ(x) — the very curve of the strip above, so the two can be compared directly. Under that, a waterfall of the time averages lays the shares end to end from 0, most dissipative first, ending at Σ = λ, with the Benettin λ as a dashed marker.

What it shows for KS

For plain KS the budget reads −∇²h ≈ +0.4, −∇⁴h ≈ −0.35, (∇h)² ≈ +0.05. The negative diffusion produces the divergence, the fourth derivative removes almost all of it, and the small remainder is the chaos. The nonlinearity's net share is small, but locally it is as large as the other two, positive on some cells and negative on others — it is the term that decides which cell splits, and thereby moves the divergence around. Note that only the average sign of a term is fixed: for a localized δh the point-wise product δh·(−∇⁴δh) is negative under the peak but positive on the flanks, so about half of the lattice points show a small positive value of the −∇⁴h contribution although the term removes seven times more than it adds.

Painting the budget on the display

The colour menu of the display offers the same fields: the total λ(x), its negative −λ(x), and λ(−∇²h), λ(−∇⁴h), λ((∇h)²), λ(α|∇h|), λ(β(∇h)⁴) for the terms present in the equation, so the budget can be painted on the profiles, the space–time map or the 2D surface. Clicking the colour bar above the display (or the button beside it in 2D) switches to grey λ<0: the colour map only where the term adds, grey to black where it removes, both signs on the same scale so their strengths can be compared. The δh(x) curve in the panel takes the same colours point for point. Since ci carries the weight δh², all these fields are concentrated where the Lyapunov vector lives and are very heavy-tailed — the few points under its peak reach a hundred times the typical value — so the colour scale is set at the 98th percentile of |c| and the rare peaks saturate. Two things stand out. For the KS equation the chaos lives only in a band around β = 0, roughly −0.07 ≤ β ≤ 0.7 (λ ≈ 0.07–0.10, with Benettin and free run agreeing to a few per cent). At β = 1 the front is already steady: the Benettin rate over 2000 time units is 0.000, and the free runs give 0.01 ± 0.02 — an occasional slow rearrangement, no chaos. β ≤ −0.5 freezes the cells (one of the four free runs at β = −0.5 caught such a rearrangement, slope 0.06, the other three gave 0.003). A small negative β (−0.2 … −0.1) lets the few cells that survive grow enormously tall — W of 33 to 55 in a box of 128 — over thousands of time units before the pattern settles; these giant-cell states are at most weakly unstable. For the ∇⁴h + ∇⁶h equation the picture is very different: the chaos survives every positive β in the list, with λ ≈ 0.08–0.10 all the way to β = 5, and only negative β of order −0.1 or beyond freezes it (at β = −0.1 three free runs gave 0 and one caught an event with slope 0.16). The chaos of the higher-order equation is thus far more robust against the slope correction than that of KS — a warning that conclusions drawn from the lowest-order KS form need not carry over to fronts whose linear instability has a different shape.

The peak at β ≈ −0.2: giant cells, a re-entrant window, and several attractors

Both curves rise again around β = −0.2 before they drop to zero at β = −0.3, which looks odd. To test whether this is a statistical accident, β = −0.3, −0.25, −0.2 and −0.15 were rerun for both equations from four different initial conditions (the default flat start with the two ε bumps; A = 0.01 with n = 7 waves; A = 1 with n = 2 waves and ε = 1; and a pure A = 1, n = 4 sine wave without bumps), each with a 4000-time-unit transient, 6000 time units of Benettin averaging (last third) and 3000 time units of repeated free runs. The peak is real and reproducible: the three generic starts agree with each other and with the table to within their scatter.

For KS the state in this band is the giant-cell front: the pattern coarsens over 2000–4000 time units into only 3–6 very tall cells (W = 20–45) that recede on average (⟨h⟩ falls at 0.1–0.2 per time unit). That front is weakly but steadily unstable, λ ≈ 0.03 from every generic start (0.025–0.041; Benettin and free run agree) — a slow, continuing rearrangement of the giant cells rather than KS-type chaos, with a growth time of some 30 time units. At β = −0.3 the default start instead stays with 14 small cells (W ≈ 4, λ ≈ 0) while two of the other starts still reach the giant-cell state with λ ≈ 0.02–0.04: at that β the two states coexist.

For the ∇⁴h + ∇⁶h equation the window is different in kind: at β = −0.2 and −0.25 the three generic starts all give an intermittent, chaotic front of 7–12 rather tall cells whose width swings between 6 and 20, with λ = 0.08–0.10 and the two fronts diverging completely in every free run — as chaotic as at β = 0. On both sides of it the front freezes: at β = −0.15 into 4 giant cells (W ≈ 10–15, λ ≈ 0.00) and at β = −0.3 into 14 small cells (W = 2.25, λ ≈ 0.00). So the higher-order equation has a re-entrant chaotic window between two frozen regimes, and the "peak" is that window sampled at one point.

The fourth start reveals a third attractor for both equations: the pure sine wave keeps its exact four-fold symmetry, and from it the front settles into a perfectly periodic steady pattern of 16 identical cells (wavelength 8, k = 0.79) with W ≈ 2–3, which is linearly stable: λ = −0.004 for KS and about −0.1 to −0.17 for the higher-order equation (in the latter case the free-run separation decays to the round-off floor within a few hundred time units, so the fitted slope reads 0 while the Benettin rate gives the true negative value). In this band of negative β the front therefore has at least three coexisting long-time states — small periodic cells, giant cells, and (for the higher-order equation) an intermittent chaotic state — and which one a run ends in depends on the initial condition. Numerics & speed
ETDRK4 integrates the stiff linear part exactly; dt is limited only by the nonlinearity. dt = 0.1 is accurate; dt = 0.25 is usually still stable. Space is spectral, so dx = 1 costs no accuracy; the dx control shrinks the lattice spacing (physical length L·dx) to resolve the same cells with more points. The time step picks itself from the strength of the nonlinear terms — 0.1 for plain KS, 0.02 up to |β| = 2, 0.01 for 2 < |β| ≤ 3.5 and 0.005 beyond (0.01 in 2D, where one step costs L times more and the measured error is already ~10⁻³), and 0.02 for the +∇⁴+∇⁶ equation — unless a finer display line spacing Δt demands a smaller one. Steps per frame are rescaled at the same time, so the animation keeps roughly its pace when dt drops. Both are yours to override here. The Lyapunov twin (panel below the display) costs a second integration per step — it runs only while that section is open; close it for full speed on big 2D lattices.

Model options

A guide to the controls. What the equation and its variants do — the physics, the Lyapunov measurements and the tables of results — is explained in the main text that pulls down from the title above the display; this menu only says what each switch, menu and button changes.

The equation in the header

The header shows the equation that is being integrated right now. Three parts of it are clickable. The linear terms toggle between −∇²h − ∇⁴h (KS) and +∇⁴h + ∇⁶h (one order higher in derivatives). The (∇h)² term is a switch: click it and it is crossed out and dropped. The switch acts on the running surface, so you can watch one equation's pattern turn into the other's without a reset. Without (∇h)² nothing stops the growth of the unstable waves, so when you switch it off β is set to 1 automatically (and back to 0 when the term returns, unless you moved β yourself in between).

Extra growth terms

Four pull-down menus in the header add optional terms to the right-hand side. Each acts immediately on the running surface. A term is "on" when its coefficient is not zero; the equation in the header always shows what is on.

α|∇h| — growth proportional to the steepness

|∇h| is the steepness of the surface (how much it tilts, whichever way). With α > 0 steep parts grow faster than flat parts; with α < 0 they grow slower. The menu offers α from −5 to +5. Beyond |α| = 2 the time step is lowered to 0.05 by itself, because the sharp ridges this term produces need finer steps. In 2D the steepness can be measured in two ways, and the small toggle next to the menu chooses: √(hx²+hy²), the usual length of the slope vector, which treats all directions alike, or |hx|+|hy|, which prefers the lattice directions — like a crystal that grows fastest along its axes. In 1D both are simply |dh/dx|.

β(∇h)⁴ — the next power of the slope

(∇h)² is the first term of a series in the slope; (∇h)⁴ is the next one. Because the fourth power is tiny where the surface is gentle and large where it is steep, this term only matters on the steep flanks of the cells. β > 0 gives the steep flanks extra growth; β < 0 holds them back. The menu runs from −5 to +5, with fine steps close to zero on the negative side where the behaviour changes quickly. The time step follows the choice (0.02 up to |β| = 2, then 0.01 and 0.005) so that the integration stays accurate.

γ∇²(∇h)² — a conserved version of the KS nonlinearity

Taking the Laplacian of (∇h)² produces a term whose average over the box is exactly zero: it moves material around on the surface but never adds any, so it does not change the mean height. Only γ < 0 gives a bounded surface; with γ > 0 the equation itself blows up in finite time (this is a property of the equation, not of the numerics), and the run stops with a warning. The time step is kept at 0.02 or below while γ is on.

δ∇²h·(∇h)² — diffusion that switches on where it is steep

∇²h alone would be ordinary diffusion, smoothing the surface. Multiplying it by (∇h)² makes the smoothing act only on the steep parts. That is why only δ ≥ 0 is offered: a negative δ would be anti-diffusion on the steep parts and blows up. The time step is kept at 0.01 or below while δ is on.

For what these terms do to the pattern and to the chaos — which of them kill it, which sign matters, and the measured Lyapunov exponents — see Variant models and the tables in the main text above the display.

Beauty — special runs

The Absolute 2D button in the presets box of the display starts a run chosen for its looks; everything is set up and the simulation starts at once. It cycles through three states: off → α = +3, h drawn upward → α = −3, drawn with −h upward (the ± button is switched on) → off. Both runs are 2D, L = 256, periodic, with the Euclidean steepness √(hx²+hy²), the surface coloured by h − ⟨h⟩ in jet in the default view (tilt 50°, rotation 0°, distance 5 L). Changing any parameter by hand returns the button to off.

Model menu

Dimension and box. 1D or 2D, box size L in lattice units (dx = 1 unless changed under Numerics), periodic or open boundaries. Periodic means the surface wraps around; open means a flat wall at each end (realised by mirroring the surface, so the slope is zero at the walls). Open runs cost 2× (1D) or 4× (2D) the work.

Initial condition. A standing wave that fits the box: n full waves (periodic) or n half waves (open) of amplitude A; in 2D the product of the x- and y-waves. Waves with k < 1 grow, so with A = 0.01 you can watch the clean exponential growth before the chaos sets in. A = 0 starts flat, and then only the disturbance (below) seeds the pattern.

Disturbance ε. Adds a small bump of height ε at two places chosen so that no mirror symmetry of the box maps the seed onto itself (1D: the strips 8–9 L/32 and 19–20 L/32; 2D: the patches x,y = 8–9 L/32 and x = 11–12, y = 23–24 L/32). This matters most for open boundaries, where a centred seed would lock the whole evolution into a mirror-symmetric pattern forever. With A = 0 the cells spread from these bumps and collide into fully generic chaos.

Numerics & speed

The time step dt and the number of steps drawn per frame. The scheme is pseudospectral (derivatives are exact in Fourier space, with 2/3-rule dealiasing of the nonlinear terms) and the time stepper is ETDRK4, a fourth-order exponential integrator that treats the stiff linear part exactly, so dt is limited only by the nonlinear terms — the extra-term menus lower it automatically when needed. Large 2D lattices (256, or open boundaries) are heavy: lower the steps per frame if the animation stutters. A run that diverges stops with a warning; reduce dt and press Reset.

Display

The view menu

The view button at the left of the toolbar above the display opens a menu over the upper left corner of the display (closed by default): the display mode, the plotted variable (1D: h or dh/dx; 2D: the field-cycling button), the vertical spacing Δy of the curves and, in the 3D modes, the Δt / spacing, tilt, rotation and distance sliders. The ⇅ button beside it switches between two viewpoints (below).

Display modes

1D: profile — the instantaneous h(x) − ⟨h⟩; profiles — the history h(x,t) as stacked curves, older ones higher up, seen from behind and above with hidden-line removal, the corner slider setting the spacing (or "auto": the true mean height); 3D, 3D sw and 3D sw* — the same surface in perspective, one curve per Δt (corner slider): 3D scrolls with the mean height, 3D sw sweeps up the page and restarts at the bottom, and 3D sw* holds the newest curve 5 % below the top of the page while the older curves slide down towards the viewer and drop off the near edge — a window that moves with the front; x–t — a space–time map, x horizontal, time upward. 2D: map — a colour map of the box; surface — a rotatable 3D view (drag to rotate, or use the tilt / rot / dist sliders of the view menu; a short distance adds perspective).

Presets

The box in the lower left corner holds the one-click runs: KS 1D / 2D, the same with α = 1 (1.5 in 2D), with β = 1.5, and the Absolute 2D toggle. Its title line names the preset that is running (presets when the run has been changed by hand), and a click on it folds the box to that line and back.

Viewpoint button

The small ⇅ button next to the view button jumps between two viewpoints: on the 2D surface between the default tilt 50°, rotation 0°, distance 5 L and tilt 45°, rotation 45°, distance 3 L; in the 1D 3D modes between distance 5 L and infinity (no perspective).

What is drawn and how it is coloured

The plotted field row of the control panel (or the plot line of the view menu) chooses the quantity that gives the height: h, the slope ∂h/∂x, the steepness |∇h| or the curvature ∇²h. The colour menu paints any of these on top of the curves or the surface, all evaluated spectrally; the display amplitude magnifies both the heights and the colour contrast. Colour schemes are grouped: diverging maps (blue–red, spectral, ice–ember) put white or grey at zero and suit signed quantities; rainbow (jet, turbo) maximise contrast; sequential maps (viridis, plasma, magma, cividis, ice, yellow, flames, grayscale) run monotonically in lightness and are the right pick for |∇h| and for print.

The ± button: plotting −h

The square ± button in the lower right corner of the display flips the sign of what is drawn: −h instead of h, so that the cells hang down instead of standing up. The slope and the curvature flip with it; the steepness |∇h| and the Lyapunov colourings do not, and the equation itself is untouched (KS is not symmetric under h → −h, which is exactly why the option is useful for comparing with pictures drawn in the other convention). The history that is already on screen is flipped with it, and the x–t map starts afresh.

Lyapunov colourings

While the Lyapunov section below the display is open, its quantities join the end of the colour menu: the local Lyapunov density λ(x), its negative, and the contribution of each term of the equation (see the main text, Which term produces the chaos?). These fields are not magnified by the display amplitude, and the colour bar shows their true range. With one of them selected the Lyapunov panel is linked: its δh(x) curve (2D: map) borrows the display's colours point by point, and a small copy of the colour scale sits in the corner of the panel. The standard / grey λ<0 button beside the short colour bar (2D), or a click on the colour bar itself (1D and 2D), paints λ > 0 with the chosen map and λ < 0 from grey to black on one common scale (the 98th percentile of |λ|, since these densities have heavy tails).

Saving pictures

The .png button in the control panel writes the display — without any control overlays — at twice the screen resolution, with the equation and its parameters stamped in the upper right corner (key p). The two small buttons in the lower right corner save clean pictures: .png keeps only the surface, without axes, frame, colour bar or caption (key c), and sq first cuts away the empty margin around the drawing and then compresses what is left into a square (key s). In the Lyapunov section and in the scan results each figure and table has its own .png button, and the tables of the main text have theirs beside them.

Lyapunov section

Open the section under the display to start the measurement: a twin surface is integrated alongside the main one and the exponent λ appears after W in the corner readout, averaged over the last two thirds of the time since the last reset or change of the equation (the window is shown in the section head). The twin doubles the work per step and runs only while the section is open. Benettin mode pulls the twin back to distance ε after every frame and shows the Lyapunov vector, the most unstable node, λloc and k̄; the terms button opens the term budget below it — one strip per term, the stacked summary and the waterfall of the averages — and Σ terms in the head must agree with λ. Free run never pulls the twin back: it plots |δh|(t) on a log scale until the two surfaces have fully diverged and fits λ as the slope; once they have diverged it also reports Dsat, the time average of |δh| on the plateau — the window within which the interface can vary (√2·W for two uncorrelated interfaces, less when the two runs stay correlated). The ↻ button restarts the twin and the averages; changing any coefficient or the linear terms restarts them automatically. What all of this means is explained in the main text (How chaotic? and Which term produces the chaos?).

Systematic scan

The Systematic scan menu repeats the measurements of the tables in the main text for an equation of your choice: set the linear terms, (∇h)² and the fixed coefficients, pick the quantity to scan — α, β, γ, δ or the box size L — with its range and step (the values −0.1, 0 and +0.1 are always included; L runs 16, 32, … up to 2n), the dimension, box, boundary, the transient (the total time per sample is twice the transient: the Benettin exponent is measured in the first half of the measurement, the free runs in the second) and the number of random initial conditions. Start sampling pauses the display and integrates in the background; the section under the Lyapunov panel fills with a table and a figure: λ (free run and Benettin, a dot per start and a line through the means), Dsat — the saturated separation of the free runs, averaged over the runs and the starts, on a log scale with √2·W dotted for comparison and a red band below the seed distance ε of the twin, where a "plateau" means the interfaces never separated — W, for an L scan also W against L on log–log axes with the fitted exponent, and the time-averaged power spectra (for a coefficient scan the largest, the smallest and the zero value; for L the largest and the fourth-largest box). Every panel has a .png button in its upper left corner that saves that panel alone; plot .png and table .png in the head save the whole figure and the table, and the circle toggles a black or white background. A value whose run diverges is marked yellow; stop ends the scan and keeps what has been measured.
