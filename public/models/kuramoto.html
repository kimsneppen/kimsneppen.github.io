Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation

This is the simplest interface equation with an intrinsic instability. Such an instability occurs whenever the parts of an interface that are ahead tend to propagate faster than the parts that lag behind. A concrete example is the fire front of a burning sheet of paper: the sections of the front that are ahead face the most unburned material, and therefore advance the fastest. To lowest order this self-amplification is the linear instability

∂h/∂t = −∇²h,

a negative diffusion under which an evolving interface grows at all wavelengths — and the shorter the wavelength, the faster the growth, without bound. A physical front must have a short-wavelength cut-off (for a flame, e.g. a minimal ignition heat), supplied by the gentlest possible regularization, −∇⁴h. Adding also the simplest possible nonlinearity, the tilt-dependent growth (∇h)², one obtains the deterministic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (KS) equation

∂h/∂t = −∇²h − ∇⁴h + (∇h)²,

where the constants in front of the three terms have been rescaled away by an appropriate scaling of space x, time t and height h. The competition between long-wavelength growth, short-wavelength damping and nonlinear mode coupling produces cells of a preferred size (λ = 2π√2 in these units) in a state of perpetual spatiotemporal chaos.

Chaos as noise: the KPZ connection. The KS equation shares the basic symmetries of the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation, and its spatiotemporal chaos acts on large scales as an emergent, self-generated noise. One therefore expects the large-scale behaviour of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation to fall in the same universality class as the KPZ equation, as explored in Refs. [3–5]. In this translation the characteristic cell has wavelength 2π/kmax, where kmax = 1/√2 is the wavenumber selected by the linearly unstable term, and the emergent large-scale dynamics carries an effective diffusion constant of about 15 (see Refs. [3–5]).

How chaotic? The largest Lyapunov exponent. Open the section below the display and the applet integrates, in parallel, a twin copy of the surface that starts a tiny distance ε = 10−6 (rms) away. After every frame it measures how far the two surfaces have drifted apart, |δh|, adds log(|δh|/ε) to a running total, and pulls the twin back to distance ε along the very same direction (Benettin's method). The total divided by the elapsed time is the largest Lyapunov exponent λ: a positive value means two nearly identical surfaces separate as eλt, so the pattern cannot be predicted beyond a time of order 1/λ; zero or negative means the state is periodic or steady. For the KS equation on a large box λ ≈ 0.09 in these units. Right after a reset the twin's separation grows at the fastest linear rate, ωmax = 1/4, and λ drops from there as the chaos saturates; the ↻ button restarts the average. The direction δh(x) that the twin keeps being pulled back along is the Lyapunov vector — the eigenvector of the most unstable direction in state space — and the panel draws it: it is localized on the cells that are just about to split or merge, the red marker sits on the most unstable node (the point where |δh| peaks), λloc is its present growth rate (the eigenvalue belonging to that vector), and k̄ is its rms wavenumber, close to the cell wavenumber 1/√2. The free run switch in the section is the long-time check: the twin is then never pulled back, and the panel plots the distance |δh| between the two interfaces on a logarithmic scale against time. A straight line means the separation grows as eλt — the exponent stays positive — all the way up to the dashed level √2·W, the distance of two completely diverged, uncorrelated interfaces, where the curve must bend over and saturate; λ is then read off as the fitted slope of the straight stretch, and the readout |δh|/√2W tells how far the divergence has come (1 = complete). Starting from ε = 10−6 with λ ≈ 0.09 this takes about ln(106)/λ ≈ 150 time units; press ↻ for another run. The twin doubles the work per step — close the section to run at full speed.

Numerics & speed
ETDRK4 integrates the stiff linear part exactly; dt is limited only by the nonlinearity. dt = 0.1 is accurate; dt = 0.25 is usually still stable. Space is spectral, so dx = 1 costs no accuracy; the dx control shrinks the lattice spacing (physical length L·dx) to resolve the same cells with more points. The time step picks itself from the strength of the nonlinear terms — 0.1 for plain KS, 0.02 up to |β| = 2, 0.01 for 2 < |β| ≤ 3.5 and 0.005 beyond (0.01 in 2D, where one step costs L times more and the measured error is already ~10⁻³), and 0.02 for the +∇⁴+∇⁶ equation — unless a finer display line spacing Δt demands a smaller one. Steps per frame are rescaled at the same time, so the animation keeps roughly its pace when dt drops. Both are yours to override here. The Lyapunov twin (panel below the display) costs a second integration per step — it runs only while that section is open; close it for full speed on big 2D lattices.
Display
Display amplitude magnifies heights and colours. The corner slider on the display sets the curve spacing Δh of the stacked profiles, or the line spacing Δt of the 3D modes. In the 3D views, tilt / rotation / distance sliders sit on the display itself (distance at the far end means no perspective); you can also drag on the 2D surface to rotate it.
About this model

The equation. ∂h/∂t = −∇²h − ∇⁴h + (∇h)² describes a growing interface h — a flame front, a solidification front, a falling liquid film. The negative diffusion term pumps energy into long wavelengths: a mode with wavenumber k grows at rate ω = k² − k⁴, so the band 0 < k < 1 is unstable, fastest at k = 1/√2 (cell size λ = 2π√2 ≈ 8.9 lattice units). The fourth derivative damps short wavelengths, and the nonlinearity (∇h)² couples the modes and saturates the growth. The result is spatiotemporal chaos: a churning pattern of cells of size ≈ 9 that perpetually split and merge.

Higher-order variant. The toggle in the header replaces the two linear terms by +∇⁴h + ∇⁶h: the instability then comes from the Laplacian of the Laplacian, with growth rate ω = k⁴ − k⁶ — the same unstable band 0 < k < 1, but flatter near k = 0 and with the fastest-growing mode at k = √(2/3) ≈ 0.82. The switch acts on the running field, so you can watch one equation's pattern relax into the other's.

Extra growth terms. The sliders in the header switch on two optional slope-dependent growth terms. α|∇h| makes the front advance in proportion to the local slope magnitude — a non-analytic term that favours facets and sharp ridges (α < 0 slows sloped regions instead). β(∇h)⁴ is the next order in the slope expansion of a growing front: β < 0 tames steep slopes and lets the cells grow taller, while β > 0 rewards steep regions with extra growth, which flattens the surface and speeds up the mean climb. The β slider runs over ±5 — in steps of 0.1 inside |β| ≤ 2 and 0.5 outside — and the time step follows it automatically (0.02 up to |β| = 2, then 0.01 and 0.005), which keeps the fourth-order integration accurate; runs stay bounded over the whole range, but if a combination ever does blow up the run stops with a warning. Both terms act instantly, without a reset.

Mean growth. Since (∇h)² ≥ 0 the mean height climbs at rate ⟨(∇h)²⟩ (KPZ-like). The display shows the fluctuation h − ⟨h⟩; the readout ⟨h⟩ tracks the climb, and W is the interface width (rms roughness).

Initial condition. A standing wave that fits the box. Periodic: h = A sin(2πn x/L), n full waves, k = 2πn/L. Open: h = A cos(πn x/L), n half waves, k = πn/L, flat at both walls. In 2D the product of the x and y waves is used. Waves with k < 1 grow; with A = 0.01 you can watch the clean exponential growth before chaos sets in.

Boundaries. Periodic uses the plain Fourier basis. Open boundaries are realised by evenly mirroring the field (cosine basis), which forces all odd derivatives to vanish at the walls; the even derivatives then vanish dynamically as the wall stays flat. Open runs cost 2× (1D) or 4× (2D) the work of periodic ones.

Numerics. Pseudospectral: derivatives are exact in Fourier space (2/3-rule dealiasing for the nonlinear term), and the time stepper is ETDRK4 (Kassam–Trefethen), a fourth-order exponential integrator that treats the stiff linear operator exactly. Large 2D lattices (256, or open boundaries) are heavy — lower steps/frame if the animation stutters.

Displays. In 1D: the instantaneous profile h(x)−⟨h⟩, the stacked profiles h(x,t) (absolute height, captured continuously; the corner slider sets the curve spacing), or the space–time map, which runs bottom-up: x horizontal, time increasing upward, like the stacked profiles. The stacked profiles use hidden-line removal — the h(x,t) surface seen from behind and above, each curve visible only where it rises over the skyline of the older ones — and scroll smoothly with the mean height. Profiles 3D renders the same surface in perspective (~60° elevation), one line every Δt set by the corner slider. In 2D: a colour map of h(x,y) filling the display, or a rotatable 3D surface steered by the tilt / rotation / distance sliders (close distance adds perspective, the far end is orthographic; drag to rotate). The display amplitude control magnifies both heights and colour contrast. Curves and maps can be coloured by h−⟨h⟩, by the slope dh/dx, by |dh/dx| (in 2D: ∂h/∂x and |∇h|), or by the curvature d²h/dx² (2D: ∇²h) — all evaluated spectrally. Colour schemes are grouped by type: diverging (blue–red, spectral, ice–ember) put white or grey at zero and are the honest choice for signed quantities; rainbow (jet, turbo) maximize contrast; sequential (viridis, plasma, magma, cividis, ice, yellow, flames, grayscale) run monotonically in lightness and are the right pick for |∇h| and for print.

Saving a picture. The .png button in the control panel writes the current display — and only the display, without any of the control overlays — to a PNG at twice the screen resolution, with the equation actually being integrated and its parameter values stamped in blue in the upper right corner. The two small buttons in the lower right corner of the display save clean pictures: .png keeps only the surface itself — no axes, tick labels, frame, colour bar or caption — at the display's aspect ratio, and sq compresses the same clean picture into a square, handy as a thumbnail or a figure panel (for the 2D colour map both are the bare square field). Keys: p, c and s.

Lyapunov exponent and vector. To measure how chaotic the running state is, the applet integrates a twin trajectory h + δh with the same scheme, started a distance ε = 10−6 (rms) away. After every frame the separation δh is measured, its growth log(|δh|/ε) is added up, and the twin is pulled back to distance ε along the same direction (Benettin's method). The running average of the growth rate is the largest Lyapunov exponent λ, shown after W in the corner readout and in the section below the display (open it to start the measurement); positive means neighbouring states separate exponentially, at a rate λ, so the pattern is unpredictable beyond a horizon of order 1/λ. For the plain KS equation on a large box λ ≈ 0.09 in these units; on a small box or in a periodic (non-chaotic) state it settles at zero or below. Right after a reset, while the state is still growing linearly from a flat start, λ approaches the maximal linear growth rate ωmax = 1/4 of the mode k = 1/√2 — watch it drop as the chaos saturates. The direction δh itself converges to the Lyapunov vector, the most unstable direction in state space: it is localized on the cells that are about to split or merge, and the panel marks the most unstable node — the lattice point where |δh| peaks — together with λloc, the current growth rate (the eigenvalue belonging to that vector, averaged over the last two time units), and the rms wavenumber k̄ of δh. The ↻ button restarts the twin and the average; changing α, β or the linear terms restarts the average automatically. The twin doubles the work per step and runs only while the section is open — close it to run at full speed. The free run mode never pulls the twin back: it records |δh|(t) on a log scale until the two interfaces have completely diverged (|δh| → √2·W, the dashed line), fits λ as the slope of the exponential stretch, and reports |δh|/√2W and the time of saturation — a check that the exponent stays positive right up to full divergence.

Disturbance. The ε toggle adds ε to the initial condition at two places: in 1D the strips 8–9 L/32 and 19–20 L/32, in 2D the patches x,y = 8–9 L/32 and x = 11–12, y = 23–24 L/32. These positions are chosen deliberately to avoid symmetries — this matters especially for open boundaries, whose mirror walls make reflection about the box centre an exact symmetry of the dynamics: a centred (or symmetrically placed) seed would then lock the entire evolution into an artificially mirror-symmetric pattern forever. The two bumps sit off-centre, at unequal distances from the two walls and from each other, so no reflection maps the seed onto itself; in 2D the second patch also breaks the x↔y diagonal symmetry that the first (diagonal) patch alone would preserve. With A = 0 the whole evolution grows from these bumps — watch their edges emit cells that invade the flat state and collide into fully generic, symmetry-free chaos.
